# 7.2 TD learning of action values: Sarsa

[Chapter 7 PDF, page 9](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%207%20Temporal-Difference%20Methods.pdf#page=9). Prerequisites: action values, the Bellman equation and one-step TD learning.

## Learning from the action actually taken

A robot is at a junction $s$, chooses action $a$, receives a reward, and reaches a new junction $s'$. To evaluate its current policy, it needs an estimate of the return after taking $a$ and then continuing with that policy. Sarsa updates that estimate as soon as the next action is selected; it need not wait for the entire episode.

The name follows the five observed quantities:

$$\underbrace{S_t}_{\text{state}},\quad\underbrace{A_t}_{\text{action}},\quad\underbrace{R_{t+1}}_{\text{reward}},\quad\underbrace{S_{t+1}}_{\text{next state}},\quad\underbrace{A_{t+1}}_{\text{next action}}.$$

Write $Q_t(s,a)$ for the current estimate of $q_\pi(s,a)$. The next action is sampled according to the policy being evaluated. For a tabular estimate, Sarsa changes the entry corresponding to the current pair:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{target}&=R_{t+1}+\gamma Q_t(S_{t+1},A_{t+1}),\\
\delta_t&=\text{target}-Q_t(S_t,A_t),\\
Q_{t+1}(S_t,A_t)&=Q_t(S_t,A_t)+\alpha_t\delta_t.
\end{aligned}$$

All other entries stay the same on this step. If $S_{t+1}$ is terminal, its future-value contribution is zero. The target uses the next *selected* action, so it depends on the policy as well as the transition.

## From the Bellman equation to a sample update

For a fixed policy $\pi$, the action-value Bellman equation says

$$q_\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E\!\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma q_\pi(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})\mid S_t=s,A_t=a\right].$$

The expectation averages possible rewards, next states and next policy actions. To see the last two sources separately, the expected continuation term is

$$\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')q_\pi(s',a').$$

The environment determines $S_{t+1}$, then the policy selects $A_{t+1}$ given that state. Sarsa replaces the expectation with the observed reward and one sampled next action, and replaces the unknown true value by the current table. The TD error measures how far this sample target lies from today's estimate. A positive error raises the entry; a negative error lowers it.

## Worked example: two possible next actions

Suppose the same observed transition starts at $(s,a)$ with $Q_t(s,a)=2$, produces reward $1$, and reaches $s'$. Let $\gamma=0.5$, $\alpha=0.1$, and the next policy select $L$ with probability $0.75$ and $R$ with probability $0.25$. The current estimates at $s'$ are $Q_t(s',L)=4$ and $Q_t(s',R)=0$.

| Next sampled action | Probability | Sarsa target | Updated $Q(s,a)$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $L$ | $0.75$ | $1+0.5(4)=3$ | $2+0.1(3-2)=2.1$ |
| $R$ | $0.25$ | $1+0.5(0)=1$ | $2+0.1(1-2)=1.9$ |

The agent makes one of these two updates, depending on the sampled next action. Their conditional mean is $0.75(2.1)+0.25(1.9)=2.05$.

**Expected Sarsa** averages over the next action instead of drawing it for the target:

$$\bar Q_t(s')=\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')Q_t(s',a')=0.75(4)+0.25(0)=3.$$

Its target is $1+0.5(3)=2.5$ and its update is $2+0.1(2.5-2)=2.05$. This equals the conditional mean of the two Sarsa updates above. Averaging removes next-action selection variability for this fixed transition; reward and next-state randomness can still affect targets across transitions. Computing the action average can also require more work when the action set is large.

## From evaluation to control

Fixed-policy evaluation asks for $q_\pi$. The book's control algorithm alternates a Sarsa update with improving the policy at the updated state. For example, an $\epsilon$-greedy policy assigns most probability to an action with the largest current $Q$, while retaining some exploration. Since the policy changes, the action values being learned and the behavior being improved evolve together.

For fixed-policy tabular evaluation, the source states almost-sure convergence with the per-pair conditions $\sum_t\alpha_t(s,a)=\infty$ and $\sum_t\alpha_t(s,a)^2<\infty$, with the usual discounted finite-MDP assumptions. A pair must be visited infinitely often for the first condition when its step size is zero on unvisited steps. The book's control illustration instead uses a constant step size and fixed $\epsilon$; those settings support its illustration but do not satisfy the stated diminishing-step-size condition. Persistent exploration also depends on reachability and does not by itself make the limiting behavior policy greedy.
