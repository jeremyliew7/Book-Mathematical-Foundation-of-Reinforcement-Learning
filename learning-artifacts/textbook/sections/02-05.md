# 2.5 Examples for illustrating the Bellman equation

A Bellman equation can look circular: the value of one state is written using another unknown value. The key is that there is **one equation for each state**, so the unknowns form a system. In this section we will build that system from the book's grid, solve it, and then change only the policy at the first state.

You need the fixed-policy Bellman expectation equation from [Section 2.4](02-04.md). The policy and environment are given throughout each example; our task is **evaluation**, not selecting an optimal action. The source uses a continuing setting with $0<\gamma<1$.

## 1. Turn a picture into one-step information

Figure 2.4 describes four states under a deterministic policy. Instead of starting with a large formula, list what happens in one step when the agent follows that policy:

| Current state | Next state | Immediate reward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $s_1$ | $s_3$ | $0$ |
| $s_2$ | $s_4$ | $1$ |
| $s_3$ | $s_4$ | $1$ |
| $s_4$ | $s_4$ | $1$ |

The last row matters: the author keeps the process going at $s_4$ and gives reward $1$ on each subsequent stay. This is not a terminal state in this calculation. If the process instead stopped on entry, the value equations would need a different terminal convention.

Let $v_i=v_\pi(s_i)$ and let $\gamma$ be the discount factor. A one-step Bellman equation is

$$
\text{current value}=\text{immediate reward}+\gamma\times\text{next-state value}.
$$

Because both the policy and transitions are deterministic here, the expectations contain only one nonzero outcome. Reading the first row gives $v_1=0+\gamma v_3$. Reading all four rows gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_1&=\gamma v_3,\\
v_2&=1+\gamma v_4,\\
v_3&=1+\gamma v_4,\\
v_4&=1+\gamma v_4.
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that $v_4$ appears on both sides. That is a relation to solve, not an instruction to replace $v_4$ by zero.

## 2. Solve the system without skipping the loop

Start with the self-loop, because its equation contains only $v_4$:

$$
v_4-\gamma v_4=1,\qquad (1-\gamma)v_4=1,\qquad v_4=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}.
$$

The division is valid because $0<\gamma<1$. Substituting this value into the $s_2$ and $s_3$ equations yields

$$
v_2=v_3=1+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}.
$$

Finally, $v_1=\gamma v_3=\gamma/(1-\gamma)$. At the book's $\gamma=0.9$, the ordered values are

$$
(v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4)=(9,10,10,10).
$$

A quick check is to substitute $v_4=10$ back into its equation: $1+0.9(10)=10$. Interpreting $v_4$ as only the next reward would produce $1$ and fail this check; the self-loop adds discounted rewards beyond the first step.

## 3. What changes when the policy is stochastic?

Figure 2.5 changes the decision at $s_1$. The policy chooses down with probability $0.5$ and right with probability $0.5$:

- Down reaches $s_3$ with immediate reward $0$.
- Right reaches $s_2$ with immediate reward $-1$.

Each action still has a deterministic environmental result in this example. The uncertainty comes from **which action the policy chooses**. Average the two one-step outcomes, including both immediate reward and discounted successor value:

$$
v_1=0.5[0+\gamma v_3]+0.5[-1+\gamma v_2].
$$

The other three equations are unchanged. Therefore $v_2=v_3=v_4=1/(1-\gamma)$ and

$$
v_1=0.5\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}
+0.5\left[-1+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right]
=-0.5+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}.
$$

At $\gamma=0.9$, this is $8.5$. Another check is to read the two branch totals using $v_2=v_3=10$: down gives $9$, right gives $8$, and their equal-weight average is $8.5$. The deterministic policy's value at $s_1$ was $9$; the $0.5$ difference comes from sometimes taking the branch with immediate reward $-1$ while the successor values match.

A common shortcut is to average the immediate rewards but forget to average the successor values as well. That happens to hide in this example because $v_2=v_3$. In a different model where successor values differ, both components must be weighted by their branch probabilities.

## Practice

1. **Self-loop.** A continuing state yields reward $2$ on every step and transitions back to itself. Let $\gamma=0.5$. Write its Bellman equation and solve for its value.

   *Hint:* isolate the value on the left, as for $v_4$.
2. **Two branches.** At state $x$, a fixed policy chooses two actions with probability $0.5$ each. Their rewards are $2$ and $-2$; both reach a state of value $4$. Let $\gamma=0.5$. Write the complete weighted equation before evaluating it.

   *Hint:* each bracket needs its own immediate reward and discounted successor value.
3. **Change an assumption.** In Figure 2.4, suppose the process terminates on reaching $s_4$ rather than continuing to pay $1$ forever. Which equation must be reconsidered, and why would the previous value at $s_4$ no longer follow automatically?

   *Hint:* compare “arrive at a target” with “keep taking actions in the target.”

## Take forward

A fixed policy and environment determine the one-step information in every row; the Bellman equations connect the unknown values. Deterministic outcomes lead to a single term, while a stochastic policy requires a probability-weighted average. [Section 2.6](02-06.md) collects the same equations into vectors and a matrix.
