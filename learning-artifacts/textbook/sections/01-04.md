# 1.4 Policy

A robot is at $s_1$. It can choose right, down, or one of the other available actions. The transition model from [Section 1.3](01-03.md) tells us what the environment may do **after** a choice. It does not choose on the robot's behalf.

A **policy** supplies the missing decision rule: given a state, how does the agent select an action? We will start with arrows, then use probabilities to express both a definite choice and a random one.

## 1. From one instruction to a rule for every state

An instruction such as “move right at $s_1$” covers only one situation. A policy specifies how to act at **each state in its domain**. The book draws a deterministic policy as an arrow at each grid cell: the arrow at a cell indicates the action chosen there. Starting from different cells and following the same policy can produce different trajectories.

A policy is not itself a trajectory. The policy is a decision rule; a trajectory is one sequence of states and actions produced when that rule is used with an environment. A single observed trajectory generally does not reveal what the policy would do in every unvisited state.

## 2. A deterministic policy as probabilities

The notation $\pi(a\mid s)$ means “the probability that the policy selects action $a$ when the current state is $s$.” In the book's deterministic example, the arrow at $s_1$ points right, which is action $a_2$:

$$
\pi(a_2\mid s_1)=1,\qquad
\pi(a_1\mid s_1)=\pi(a_3\mid s_1)=\pi(a_4\mid s_1)=\pi(a_5\mid s_1)=0.
$$

There is no randomness in the action chosen at this state: the one selected action has probability one. This does **not** imply that the environment's response must be deterministic; the transition model is a separate rule.

## 3. A stochastic policy is still a rule

Figure 1.5 uses a different policy at $s_1$: choose right or down with equal probability. The corresponding row of the book's policy table is

| State | $a_1$ up | $a_2$ right | $a_3$ down | $a_4$ left | $a_5$ stay |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $s_1$ | 0 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0 | 0 |

Thus $\pi(a_2\mid s_1)=0.5$ and $\pi(a_3\mid s_1)=0.5$. Randomness is part of the rule: the policy specifies a probability distribution over actions. On any one visit the agent chooses one action, not half of each action.

For any fixed state $s$, a valid policy has

$$
\pi(a\mid s)\geq 0\quad\text{for each available }a,
\qquad
\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}\pi(a\mid s)=1.
$$

In this book's grid, $\mathcal A(s)=\{a_1,\ldots,a_5\}$ for every state. The probabilities in the example row are nonnegative and sum to $1$. Other rows can have different action probabilities; the whole table, not just this row, represents the policy.

**Pause and predict:** Suppose an agent uses the stochastic row above at $s_1$. Can it choose right on two consecutive visits to $s_1$? Consider the meaning of a probability on each visit; do not assume that a row prescribes an alternating sequence.

## 4. Policy and transition: two questions in sequence

Both objects use conditional probabilities, so it is easy to confuse their inputs and outputs:

| Object | What is given? | What probability does it describe? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Policy $\pi(a\mid s)$ | Current state $s$ | The agent chooses action $a$ |
| Transition $p(s'\mid s,a)$ | Current state $s$ and chosen action $a$ | The environment produces next state $s'$ |

To see how they work together, use the book's policy row above and its deterministic grid transition rules:

1. At $s_1$, the policy chooses right $a_2$ with probability $0.5$ or down $a_3$ with probability $0.5$.
2. Under Table 1.1, right from $s_1$ leads to $s_2$, while down leads to $s_4$.
3. Therefore, in this particular combination of policy and environment, the next state is $s_2$ with probability $0.5$ and $s_4$ with probability $0.5$.

The policy probabilities and transition outcomes are from the book; the combined two-step calculation is an explanatory addition. The random next state in this example comes from the random **action choice**, even though the environment's transition rule is deterministic. A stochastic environment could introduce further randomness after the action has been chosen.

## Practice

1. A policy row for five available actions is $(0,0.2,0.3,0,0.5)$. Check whether it is a valid distribution and identify the actions with positive probability.
2. At a different state, suppose the row becomes $(0,0,1,0,0)$. Is the policy's choice at that state deterministic? Explain what the entries mean.
3. An agent changes its policy but the environment's transition probabilities stay fixed. Which part of the two-stage decision process changes?
4. In the book's deterministic grid, assume a new policy chooses $a_2$ at $s_1$ with probability $0.25$ and $a_3$ with probability $0.75$, with all other action probabilities zero. What are the possible next states and their probabilities? Show both stages.

## Take forward

A policy describes **action selection given the current state**. The transition model describes **the next-state distribution given the state and selected action**. Together they help describe how the agent moves through the environment; Section 1.5 adds the feedback received along the way.
