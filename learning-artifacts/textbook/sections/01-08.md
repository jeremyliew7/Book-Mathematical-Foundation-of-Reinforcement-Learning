# 1.8 Summary: follow one decision through the loop

[Chapter 1 PDF, page 12](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%201%20Basic%20Concepts.pdf#page=12), book page 12. The source summary is brief; this companion uses the original corridor example from [1.6](01-06.md) and [1.7](01-07.md) to connect the chapter's ideas. Prerequisites: Sections 1.1–1.7.

## The question

A robot starts at $A$. Its policy chooses **go** with probability $0.75$ and **wait** with probability $0.25$. The environment sends it to $B$ with probability $0.8$ after go, but never after wait. What is the chance that the next state is $B$? What can we conclude from one observed trajectory?

Separate the choices from the environment: $0.75$ is $\pi(\mathrm{go}\mid A)$; $0.8$ is $p(B\mid A,\mathrm{go})$. By the law of total probability,

$$P_\pi(B\mid A)=0.75(0.8)+0.25(0)=0.6.$$

This is a probability for a possible next state, not the value of a particular run. A realized trajectory either reaches $B$ on that step or does not. If one run fails first, then succeeds, and finally reaches terminal state $T$, its reward list in Section 1.6 is $-1,0,2$. With $\gamma=0.5$, that run's discounted return is $-1+0.5(0)+0.5^2(2)=-0.5$.

## The objects and their jobs

| Object | What it answers | In this example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State | What situation is represented now? | $A$, $B$, or $T$ |
| Action | What choice is made? | go or wait where legal |
| Policy | How is an action selected at a state? | $\pi(\mathrm{go}\mid A)=0.75$ |
| Transition | What next-state distribution follows a state and action? | $p(B\mid A,\mathrm{go})=0.8$ |
| Reward | What feedback follows the step? | $-1$ on a failed go in this constructed model |
| Return | How are rewards aggregated along a trajectory? | $-0.5$ in the particular discounted run above |

A policy is a decision rule, a trajectory is a record, and a return is a quantity calculated from that record. The agent need not already know the environment model. Separate transition and reward marginals do not, by themselves, specify their joint relationship; see [Section 1.7](01-07.md).

## What remains unknown?

The step probability $0.6$ does not tell us the complete long-run performance of the policy. Nor does a single return of $-0.5$ establish its expected return: different action and transition samples can produce different trajectories. Chapter 2 introduces expected return from a state under a specified policy. We have not calculated that expectation here.

The Markov question also remains separate from the numerical calculation. Position is an adequate state only when the modeled current state and action contain the information needed for the relevant conditional predictions. If unrecorded velocity affects the next step, position alone may fail that test.

## Review and transfer

1. **Recognize.** In the loop, what is selected by the policy and what is produced by the environment? *Hint:* compare $\pi(a\mid s)$ with $p(s'\mid s,a)$.
2. **Apply.** If the same failed-then-successful run has $\gamma=1$ and ends at $T$, form its return. *Hint:* add its three rewards without discounting.
3. **Reason.** Why does knowing one run's return not establish the expected return of a stochastic policy? *Hint:* compare a realization with a distribution.
4. **Extend.** Imagine the policy stays the same but the corridor becomes slippery, changing the probability of reaching $B$ after go. Which object in the table changes? *Hint:* distinguish decision rule from dynamics.
