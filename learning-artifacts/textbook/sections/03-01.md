# 3.1 From evaluating a policy to improving one

[Chapter 3 PDF, pages 2–3](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%203%20Optimal%20State%20Values%20and%20Bellman%20Optimality%20Equation.pdf#page=2), book pages 36–37. Section 3.2 starts partway through page 3. Prerequisites: fixed-policy state and action values from Chapter 2. We will reproduce the source's action comparison step by step, then use a smaller independent example to see what changes after a policy is improved.

## The question we could not answer with evaluation alone

Chapter 2 asks: **if a particular policy $\pi$ keeps being followed, what return should we expect from each state?** Its answer is $v_\pi(s)$. This chapter asks a different question: **after evaluating $\pi$, can another action lead to a better policy?**

An action cannot be judged from immediate reward alone. A costly first step may lead to a valuable future state, while a tempting immediate reward may lead somewhere poor. We therefore need to calculate the first action *and* what happens after it.

## Rebuild the author's fixed-policy values

In the book's Figure 3.2, $s_4$ is the target and the fixed policy $\pi$ keeps receiving $+1$ there. The relevant policy transitions and rewards are:

| Current state | Next state under $\pi$ | Immediate reward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $s_1$ | $s_2$ | $-1$ |
| $s_2$ | $s_4$ | $+1$ |
| $s_3$ | $s_4$ | $+1$ |
| $s_4$ | $s_4$ | $+1$ |

With discount $\gamma=0.9$, start at $s_4$. The fixed-policy Bellman equation is

$$v_\pi(s_4)=1+0.9v_\pi(s_4).$$

Subtract $0.9v_\pi(s_4)$ from both sides. This gives $0.1v_\pi(s_4)=1$, so $v_\pi(s_4)=10$. The agent does **not** terminate immediately at $s_4$ in this example; the repeated $+1$ matters. Next, substitute that result into the other state equations:

$$\begin{aligned}
v_\pi(s_2)&=1+0.9(10)=10,\\
v_\pi(s_3)&=1+0.9(10)=10,\\
v_\pi(s_1)&=-1+0.9(10)=8.
\end{aligned}$$

These numbers evaluate the **old** policy. They are not yet values for a new policy.

## Compare one action, then return to the old policy

The action value $q_\pi(s,a)$ asks what happens if the first action is fixed as $a$ at $s$ and the old policy $\pi$ is followed afterwards. In the source example:

| First action at $s_1$ | Immediate reward | Next state | Continue with old-policy value | $q_\pi(s_1,a)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $a_1$ up | $-1$ | $s_1$ | $v_\pi(s_1)=8$ | $-1+0.9(8)=6.2$ |
| $a_2$ right | $-1$ | $s_2$ | $v_\pi(s_2)=10$ | $-1+0.9(10)=8$ |
| $a_3$ down | $0$ | $s_3$ | $v_\pi(s_3)=10$ | $0+0.9(10)=9$ |
| $a_4$ left | $-1$ | $s_1$ | $v_\pi(s_1)=8$ | $-1+0.9(8)=6.2$ |
| $a_5$ stay | $0$ | $s_1$ | $v_\pi(s_1)=8$ | $0+0.9(8)=7.2$ |

The source's greatest score is $9$, so it changes the decision at $s_1$ from right to down. This comparison uses $q_\pi$, not $q_{\pi'}$: $\pi$ still determines the continuation in every row. Calculating a one-step score is not the same as knowing the full value of the new policy $\pi'$.

## A smaller check: evaluate the improved policy again

The following **independent** two-state MDP will return in Sections [3.3](03-03.md) and [3.4](03-04.md). Let $\gamma=0.5$; transitions in this table are deterministic.

| State | Action | Reward | Next state |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $A$ | Left | $1$ | $B$ |
| $A$ | Right | $0.5$ | $A$ |
| $B$ | Wait | $0.2$ | $B$ |

Let old policy $\pi$ choose Right at $A$ and Wait at $B$. First evaluate it:

$$v_\pi(A)=0.5+0.5v_\pi(A)=1,\qquad v_\pi(B)=0.2+0.5v_\pi(B)=0.4.$$

Then compare the **first** action at $A$, retaining $\pi$ afterwards:

$$q_\pi(A,\mathrm{Left})=1+0.5(0.4)=1.2,\qquad q_\pi(A,\mathrm{Right})=0.5+0.5(1)=1.$$

The greedy choice is Left. Define $\pi'$ to choose Left at $A$ and Wait at $B$. Re-evaluating $\pi'$ gives $v_{\pi'}(B)=0.4$ and $v_{\pi'}(A)=1+0.5(0.4)=1.2$: for this constructed example its value improves at $A$ and stays the same at $B$.

## Why this is a bridge, not the destination

It is tempting to say “the new value is the maximum $q_\pi$” and stop. That maximum describes a first action followed by the **old** policy; the new policy may act differently again at later states. General guarantees need assumptions and proof. Section 3.2 defines what “optimal at every state” means; Sections 3.3–3.4 show how the Bellman optimality equation and its fixed point establish an optimal policy in the finite discounted setting.

## Try it yourself

1. **Recognize.** In $q_\pi(s,a)$, which policy is used after the specified first action? *Hint:* the subscript identifies the continuation policy.
2. **Calculate.** If action A gives $-2$ then reaches a state valued $10$ under $\pi$, and action B gives $1$ then reaches a state valued $4$, compare their one-step scores for $\gamma=0.5$. *Hint:* calculate reward plus discounted continuation for each action separately.
3. **Reason.** Why does taking the largest $q_\pi$ at one state not, by itself, prove a newly defined policy is globally optimal? *Hint:* list what happens at the other states and distinguish evaluation from optimality.
4. **Extend.** In the two-state example, keep the old policy but change Left's immediate reward from $1$ to $0.2$. Which first-action score changes, and what extra step is necessary before stating the value of an improved policy? *Hint:* changing a model quantity and re-evaluating a changed policy are different operations.
