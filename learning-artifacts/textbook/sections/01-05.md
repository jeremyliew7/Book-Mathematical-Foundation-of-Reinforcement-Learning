# 1.5 Reward

The robot has a state, chooses an action, and the environment produces a next state. But how does the agent receive a signal that some behavior is desirable? The book introduces **reward**: numerical feedback associated with a step.

We will use the grid's actual reward rules, then examine why a favorable immediate reward may not settle a long-term decision. A reward is not necessarily positive, and a reward signal does not automatically describe everything a designer cares about.

## 1. Read one step of feedback

In the book's deterministic grid, reward is written $r(s,a)$: the number received after choosing action $a$ in state $s$. The author chooses the following values for this example:

| Event | Immediate reward |
| --- | --- |
| Attempt to cross the grid boundary | $-1$ |
| Attempt to enter a forbidden cell | $-1$ |
| Reach the target | $+1$ |
| Other cases | $0$ |

These numbers are part of this **particular environment's design**. Reinforcement learning does not require a boundary penalty of $-1$ or a target reward of $+1$ in every task.

Consider $s_1$, the top-left cell. Choosing $a_1$ (up) attempts to cross the boundary. The transition rule leaves the robot at $s_1$; the reward rule gives $-1$. We needed both rules: the transition tells us **where the robot ends up**, and the reward tells us **what numerical feedback accompanies the step**.

## 2. Same next state, different feedback

The book makes an important modeling choice: reaching target state $s_9$ does not have to end the process. Suppose the agent is already at $s_9$:

- Choose $a_5$ (stay): the next state is $s_9$, and the book gives reward $+1$.
- Choose $a_2$ (right): the agent hits the boundary, the next state is still $s_9$, and the book gives reward $-1$.

The next state is the same in both cases, but the rewards differ because the actions and associated rules differ. Therefore, in this example, knowing only the next state is insufficient to recover the reward.

**Pause and predict:** If a model ends the episode immediately on reaching $s_9$, would these subsequent choices at $s_9$ be part of that episode? Check the stopping rule before comparing the two scenarios.

## 3. Why not always pick the largest immediate reward?

One step of feedback is only one part of a trajectory. To see the distinction, consider this **original two-step illustration**, not the book's grid. Assume the two actions lead to the shown consequences, the task then ends, and we compare the undiscounted sum of both rewards:

| First choice | Immediate reward | Forced next reward | Two-step total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | $+2$ | $-5$ | $-3$ |
| B | $0$ | $+1$ | $+1$ |

Choice A wins if we look only at the first reward. Choice B wins under the explicitly stated two-step total. The result follows from the future consequences and the comparison rule, not from the action labels. Changing the horizon or discounting can change the comparison.

The accumulated quantity is called **return**; the book develops trajectories and return in [Section 1.6](01-06.md). At this point the important distinction is simply *one-step feedback* versus *the rewards accumulated over a specified future*.

## 4. When the feedback is uncertain

The book's Table 1.3 records a single reward for each state–action pair. That representation fits its deterministic reward example. A general model can instead describe a distribution:

$$
p(r\mid s,a)=\Pr(R_{t+1}=r\mid S_t=s,A_t=a).
$$

For example, the book's boundary rule gives $p(r=-1\mid s_1,a_1)=1$. That says the reward is $-1$ with certainty **under those stated conditions**.

As a separate, hypothetical example, imagine that the same action at the same state gives $+1$ with probability $0.4$ and $0$ with probability $0.6$. One execution produces one of those rewards. Their conditional mean is $0.4(1)+0.6(0)=0.4$, but $0.4$ is not the observed reward on either outcome in this example. The distribution contains more information than its mean alone.

A reward distribution does not by itself specify the next-state distribution. In a general environment, reward and next state can be related; this section does not assume they are independent.

## Practice

1. In the book's target-state example, identify the two actions that lead to the same next state but different rewards. Which rule explains the difference?
2. Consider a two-step task: action A yields $+3$ then $-4$; action B yields $0$ then $+2$. If the objective is the undiscounted two-step total, which has the larger total? Show the addition.
3. A particular state–action pair yields reward $2$ with probability $0.25$ and reward $0$ otherwise. What is its expected immediate reward? Is that expectation necessarily one of the possible observed rewards?
4. Give an example of a behavior that might earn a favorable immediate score while still conflicting with the designer's longer-term goal. What additional information would be needed to evaluate the behavior?

## Take forward

Reward is feedback for a step; return summarizes rewards over a specified sequence and horizon. A transition rule, reward rule, and episode-ending rule answer different questions. Section 1.6 connects them by writing down actual trajectories and their returns.
