# 3.5 What can change an optimal policy?

[Chapter 3 PDF, pages 15–19](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%203%20Optimal%20State%20Values%20and%20Bellman%20Optimality%20Equation.pdf#page=15), book pages 49–53. Prerequisites: discounted return, the Bellman optimality operator, and the all-state definition of an optimal policy. Source figures describe the author's grid world; the other examples are constructed for this lesson.

## A decision changes when the problem changes

An optimal policy depends on the reward rules, transition dynamics, and discount factor. It is tempting to treat an optimal action as a property of a state alone, but it is optimal **for a specified model and objective**. Change one of those inputs and the same action need not remain optimal.

The source compares its grid-world policies with different discounts and with a larger forbidden-cell penalty. In its particular Figure 3.4, changing $\gamma$ from $0.9$ to $0.5$ changes the routes; changing the forbidden penalty from $-1$ to $-10$ also changes them. These observations belong to that example. A smaller discount emphasizes nearer rewards, but does not by itself determine what every environment's optimal route must be.

## Discount: a small calculation where the preferred action flips

At a starting state, suppose **Quick** gives reward $2$ immediately and then terminates, while **Wait** gives reward $0$ now, then reward $3$ at the next step and terminates. There are no other rewards. Compare their returns:

| Discount | Quick | Wait | Higher return |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\gamma=0$ | $2$ | $0+0(3)=0$ | Quick |
| $\gamma=0.5$ | $2$ | $0+0.5(3)=1.5$ | Quick |
| $\gamma=0.9$ | $2$ | $0+0.9(3)=2.7$ | Wait |

The table is an original deterministic counterexample to “the optimal action must stay the same when $\gamma$ changes.” The policy flips because the later reward is weighted differently.

The book's Figure 3.5 gives another, continuing example. Reaching its target immediately gives reward $1$ every step thereafter, while a detour first gives two zero rewards and then that same stream. Their returns are $1/(1-\gamma)$ and $\gamma^2/(1-\gamma)$. At $\gamma=0.9$ they are $10$ and $8.1$. Discounting makes the delay costly **under those specific streams**; it does not prove shortest routes are optimal for arbitrary rewards.

## Which reward change preserves an optimal policy?

The source's Theorem 3.6 considers a continuing infinite-horizon discounted problem with $0<\gamma<1$. Apply the **same** transformation to the reward on every time step, including an absorbing state's subsequent rewards:

$$R'_{t+1}=\alpha R_{t+1}+\beta,\qquad \alpha>0.$$

For any fixed policy and starting state, expand its transformed return:

$$\begin{aligned}
G'_0&=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t(\alpha R_{t+1}+\beta)\\
&=\alpha\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^tR_{t+1}+\beta\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t\\
&=\alpha G_0+\frac{\beta}{1-\gamma}.
\end{aligned}$$

The first equality substitutes the transformation. The second separates two convergent series; the third uses the geometric-series sum. Taking expected values gives

$$v'_\pi(s)=\alpha v_\pi(s)+\frac{\beta}{1-\gamma}.$$

The same positive scale and the same constant shift apply to **every** policy from a given state. Therefore, if $v_{\pi_1}(s)>v_{\pi_2}(s)$ before the change, the inequality still holds afterwards; ties also remain ties. Applying this to every state gives the source's optimal-value relationship $v'^*=\alpha v^*+\frac{\beta}{1-\gamma}\mathbf 1$ and preserves the optimal-policy set.

### Check the transformation on our two-state model

Use the independent model from [Section 3.3](03-03.md): at $A$, Left gives $1$ then reaches $B$, Right gives $0.5$ then returns to $A$; at $B$, Wait gives $0.2$ and stays at $B$. With $\gamma=0.5$, the verified optimal value is $(v^*(A),v^*(B))=(1.2,0.4)$.

Let $\alpha=2$ and $\beta=3$. The common shift is $3/(1-0.5)=6$, so the predicted new values are $(8.4,6.8)$. The transformed immediate rewards are $5$ for Left, $4$ for Right, and $3.4$ for Wait. Check each score:

$$q'(A,\mathrm{Left})=5+0.5(6.8)=8.4,\quad q'(A,\mathrm{Right})=4+0.5(8.4)=8.2,$$

$$q'(B,\mathrm{Wait})=3.4+0.5(6.8)=6.8.$$

Left remains greedy at $A$, and the transformed values satisfy the fixed-point equations.

## Why “add a constant” needs a horizon condition

The source compares a global continuing reward shift with adding a step penalty. Those are not always the same operation. Suppose an episodic task stops assigning rewards at termination, and the added $\beta$ is paid only on steps **before** termination. For an episode length $T$, the shift becomes

$$\beta\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t=\beta\frac{1-\gamma^T}{1-\gamma},$$

which depends on $T$. If policies can have different episode lengths, they can receive different total shifts.

For a concrete counterexample at $\gamma=0.9$, let Quick give reward $1$ then terminate; let Wait give $0$ now and $0$ next step, then terminate. Initially Quick has return $1$ and Wait has $0$. Add $\beta=2$ **only to the existing pre-termination rewards**: Quick now gives $3$, while Wait gives $2+0.9(2)=3.8$. Their ranking reverses. If instead the same $+2$ were also paid forever after both entered an absorbing continuation, the global continuing theorem would apply and this reversal would not follow.

Changing only one penalty, such as the forbidden-cell reward, is also not the global transformation: other rewards have not received the same $\alpha r+\beta$ change. This is why the source's grid-world penalty experiment does not contradict its invariance theorem.

## Try it yourself

1. **Recognize.** In the Quick/Wait table, which reward is multiplied by $\gamma$? *Hint:* compare its time index with the first reward.
2. **Apply.** For $\gamma=0.5$, $\alpha=1$, $\beta=-1$, calculate the common shift $\beta/(1-\gamma)$. *Hint:* the shift is the sum of the same added reward over all time steps.
3. **Reason.** Why is changing only the forbidden penalty not covered by a transformation applied to every reward? *Hint:* check an unchanged reward against the same proposed $\alpha,\beta$.
4. **Extend.** In the episodic counterexample, what would change in the comparison if the added reward continued forever after termination in an absorbing state? *Hint:* write the complete added reward stream for each policy.
