# 10.3 Off-policy actor-critic

[Chapter 10 PDF, page 7](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%2010%20Actor-Critic%20Methods.pdf#page=7). Prerequisites: A2C, conditional expectation and the policy-gradient theorem.

## Two policies, two questions

A robot collected experience with an exploratory **behavior policy** $\beta$. We now want to improve a different **target policy** $\pi_\theta$. At a visited state, can we reuse the behavior action to estimate a target-policy expectation? And do those visited states have the frequency required by the target objective? The answers involve action weights and state weights, respectively.

## Importance sampling at one state

For discrete distributions $p_0$ and $p_1$, if $p_1(x)>0$ wherever $p_0(x)>0$, then

$$\mathbb E_{p_0}[f(X)]=\sum_{x:\,p_1(x)>0} p_1(x)\frac{p_0(x)}{p_1(x)}f(x)=\mathbb E_{p_1}\!\left[\frac{p_0(X)}{p_1(X)}f(X)\right].$$

For an action sampled under behavior, $\beta(a\mid s)>0$, so its weight $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)/\beta(a\mid s)$ is defined. The support condition ensures the behavior can sample every target-supported action. Rare behavior actions with large target probability receive large weights, which can make estimates variable.

In the source's $X\in\{+1,-1\}$ example, $p_0=(0.5,0.5)$ and $p_1=(0.8,0.2)$. The two weights are $0.5/0.8=0.625$ and $0.5/0.2=2.5$. Under $p_1$ the expected weighted sample is

$$0.8(0.625)(+1)+0.2(2.5)(-1)=0,$$

matching the $p_0$ mean. If $p_1(-1)=0$, no behavior sample contains the missing contribution: the support condition fails.

## The exact state-weighted gradient

The book fixes $\beta$ with stationary state distribution $d_\beta$, and studies $J(\theta)=\sum_s d_\beta(s)v_{\pi_\theta}(s)$ for discount $0<\gamma<1$. The initial-state distribution $d_\beta$ is fixed while $\theta$ changes. Under the theorem's differentiability and support assumptions, expanding the value-gradient recursion produces

$$\rho_\theta(s)=\sum_xd_\beta(x)\sum_{k=0}^\infty\gamma^k[P_{\pi_\theta}^k]_{xs}.$$

For a fixed behavior policy, assume target support is covered in a neighborhood of the parameter, and the policy is differentiable. The exact gradient can be expressed without logarithms as

$$\nabla_\theta J=\sum_s\rho_\theta(s)\sum_{a:\,\beta(a\mid s)>0}\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q_{\pi_\theta}(s,a).$$

When $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)>0$ for every behavior-supported action in this sum, use $\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta=\pi_\theta\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta$ to obtain the score form:

$$\nabla_\theta J=\sum_s\rho_\theta(s)\sum_{a:\,\beta(a\mid s)>0}\beta(a\mid s)\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\beta(a\mid s)}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q_{\pi_\theta}(s,a).$$

The extra positivity condition makes the logarithm and score meaningful. A target action probability of zero is allowed by ordinary importance-sampling support, but its log score is undefined; use the differentiable-policy form or a theorem appropriate to the boundary. Actions with both probabilities zero are omitted from the behavior-supported sum.

The inner ratio changes the action expectation at a fixed state. The outer weight counts discounted visits under the **target** policy starting from $d_\beta$. Summing $\rho_\theta$ over all states gives $\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k=1/(1-\gamma)$, so it is an unnormalized measure. If states are sampled from $(1-\gamma)\rho_\theta$, multiply the expectation by $1/(1-\gamma)$ to recover the displayed gradient. The source writes an expectation over $\rho$; this normalization follows from its displayed definition.

### Why an action ratio does not fix state frequencies

Consider two states $s_0,s_1$, and a behavior chain with transition probabilities $0.1$ from $s_0$ to $s_1$ and $0.9$ from $s_1$ to $s_0$. Its stationary distribution is $d_\beta=(0.9,0.1)$. Suppose the target policy always moves to $s_1$ and stays there; the required transitions are supported by the behavior policy. With $\gamma=0.9$, the initial contribution to $\rho$ is $(0.9,0.1)$, and from step 1 onward all target occupancy is at $s_1$:

$$\rho=(0.9,\;0.1+0.9+0.9^2+\cdots)=(0.9,9.1).$$

After normalization, the target discounted state weights are $(0.09,0.91)$, very different from the behavior frequencies $(0.9,0.1)$. This deterministic target illustrates occupancy; the positive-target-probability requirement above is needed to use the score form. Reweighting which action was selected at $s_0$ or $s_1$ does not change how often those states were observed.

## Book algorithm: one-step weighted updates

Write $c_t=\pi_{\theta_t}(a_t\mid s_t)/\beta(a_t\mid s_t)$ and $\delta_t=r_{t+1}+\gamma v_{w_t}(s_{t+1})-v_{w_t}(s_t)$. Algorithm 10.3 uses behavior actions and updates

$$\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t+\alpha_\theta c_t\delta_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta_t}(a_t\mid s_t),\qquad
w_{t+1}=w_t+\alpha_wc_t\delta_t\nabla_wv_{w_t}(s_t).$$

For the displayed log-score actor update, the sampled target probability must be positive. The ratio appears in both updates. The one-step action correction does not by itself make the behavior-state weighting equal to the theorem's target occupancy; connecting these sampled steps to that exact gradient requires additional state-weight conditions or correction. A learned value function also influences the quality of the TD signal.

The Springer errata changes the target parameter in this actor update from $\theta$ to $\theta_t$; the GitHub PDF already includes that correction.
