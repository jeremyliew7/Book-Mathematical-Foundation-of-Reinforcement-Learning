# 5.1 From a sample average to a value estimate

**Source:** [Chapter 5 PDF, pages 2–4](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%205%20Monte%20Carlo%20Methods.pdf#page=2), printed pages 78–80. This independent companion adds a running example used throughout Sections 5.1–5.3.

You already know that a value is an expected return. This section answers a practical question: if the return distribution is unknown, what can observations tell us about its mean? By the end, you should be able to compute a sample mean, distinguish it from the target mean, and explain which assumptions support the calculation.

## A small decision room

An agent is in state $C$. It can choose **safe** and end the episode with reward $+1$, choose **risky** and end with reward $+3$, or choose **wait**, receive $-1$, and return to $C$. The discount factor is $\gamma=0.5$. For now suppose the fixed policy $\pi$ chooses safe with probability $1/2$, risky with $1/4$, and wait with $1/4$ each time it is in $C$.

The return from time $t$ is $G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\cdots$ until the episode ends. The state value $v_\pi(C)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_0\mid S_0=C]$ is the **population mean** of these returns, not a single episode's return. The episode ends almost surely under this policy because the chance of waiting $n$ consecutive times is $(1/4)^n$.

For a concrete record, consider three independently generated episodes under this unchanged policy:

| Episode | Actions | Rewards | Discounted return from $C$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | safe | $1$ | $1$ |
| 2 | risky | $3$ | $3$ |
| 3 | wait, risky | $-1,3$ | $-1+0.5(3)=0.5$ |

These are illustrative possible observations. Each episode begins with the policy choosing an action; we are estimating a **state value**, not forcing a specified first action.

## Known distribution versus observed returns

If the return $X$ takes values $x$ with known probabilities $p(x)$, its mean is $\mu=\mathbb E[X]=\sum_x p(x)x$. A known model can provide those probabilities; it is unnecessary for the sample-average calculation below.

Here the model is provided only so that we can check the target. Conditioning on the first action gives

$$
v_\pi(C)=\tfrac12(1)+\tfrac14(3)+\tfrac14(-1+0.5v_\pi(C))
=1+\tfrac18v_\pi(C)=\tfrac87.
$$

The safe and risky branches end immediately; after wait, the agent returns to $C$ and follows the same policy. Thus the continuation has value $v_\pi(C)$ and is discounted once. The final equality follows by subtracting $v_\pi(C)/8$ from both sides.

In model-free estimation, pretend that this target calculation is unavailable. Given $n$ sampled returns $G_1,\ldots,G_n$, use

$$
\hat v_n(C)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_i.
$$

Here $i$ indexes episodes, $G_i$ is the entire discounted return in episode $i$, and $n$ is the number of sampled episodes. For the record above, the successive estimates are $1$, $(1+3)/2=2$, and $(1+3+0.5)/3=1.5$. The third estimate is not equal to the true $8/7$; also, adding a sample did not make every intermediate estimate closer to the target. An estimate can move either way as observations arrive.

## Why averaging can work

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) copies of $X$, with finite variance $\sigma^2$ and mean $\mu$. For the random estimator $\bar X_n=n^{-1}\sum_i X_i$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[\bar X_n]
&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb E[X_i]
=\frac{n\mu}{n}=\mu,\\
\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)
&=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_i)
=\frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Linearity of expectation gives the first sum; identical distributions give each mean $\mu$. Independence removes cross-covariance terms in the variance sum, and identical distributions give each variance $\sigma^2$. Unbiasedness says that the estimator averages to $\mu$ **across hypothetical repeated datasets**; it does not say the one observed estimate equals $\mu$. Under these assumptions its variance shrinks, and the sample mean converges to $\mu$ in probability as $n$ grows.

Independence and identical distribution are sufficient here, not necessary for every possible convergence result. If all observations copy the first one, repeating them does not remove its error. Likewise, observations from a different policy need not estimate the same value.

**Checkpoint:** Which of the following changes the target: observing one more episode under $\pi$, or changing $\pi$ before collecting the next episode? Why?

## Practice

1. **Calculate.** Two fresh episodes return $1$ and $0.5$. Compute their sample mean. Does it have to equal $8/7$?
   - Hint 1: Add the two returns; then divide by the number of episodes.
2. **Explain.** In the variance calculation, which step needs independence and which needs identical distributions?
   - Hint 1: Expand the variance of a sum and look for covariance terms.
   - Hint 2: Compare the individual means and variances.
3. **Transfer.** Someone collects 100 copies of the same recorded episode and treats them as 100 independent episodes. Explain what information the copies add and why the $1/n$ variance formula is inappropriate.
