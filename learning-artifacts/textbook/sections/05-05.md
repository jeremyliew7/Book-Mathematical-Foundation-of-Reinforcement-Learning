# 5.5 Exploration and exploitation

**Source:** [Chapter 5 PDF, pages 16–21](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%205%20Monte%20Carlo%20Methods.pdf#page=16), printed pages 92–97. The numerical room below continues [Section 5.4](05-04.md).

**Exploitation** favors the action that currently has the highest estimated value. **Exploration** tries actions to improve the information available for later decisions. A low estimate may reflect poor outcomes, or simply too few observations to identify a better action.

## How exploration changes both experience and return

The room has safe ($+1$, then terminal), risky ($+3$, then terminal), and wait ($-1$, then back to $C$), with $\gamma=0.5$. Suppose risky is the designated greedy action and $\epsilon=0.3$. From Section 5.4 the action probabilities are $0.8$, $0.1$, $0.1$ for risky, safe, wait respectively.

We can calculate this policy's value when the room dynamics are known. Let $v$ be the expected return at $C$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v&=0.8(3)+0.1(1)+0.1(-1+0.5v)\\
 &=2.4+0.05v,\\
v&=\frac{2.4}{0.95}=\frac{48}{19}\approx2.526.
\end{aligned}
$$

The wait branch earns $-1$ immediately, then returns to the same state with discounted continuation value $0.5v$. An always-risky policy has value $3$. Keeping $\epsilon=0.3$ gives safe and wait a chance to be sampled, but also chooses them with positive probability when risky is already known to be best.

For comparison, $\epsilon=1$ makes all three actions equally likely. Its value solves $v=(3+1-1+0.5v)/3$, giving $v=6/5$. This example shows the tradeoff in a setting where action outcomes are known; during learning, exploration can reveal information that changes which action is designated greedy.

## A finite-sample view

Imagine safe is currently designated greedy, $\epsilon=0.3$, and each of ten independent room starts draws its first action under the same fixed policy. Risky then has probability $0.1$ on each start. The chance it is selected at least once is

$$
1-(1-0.1)^{10}=1-0.9^{10}\approx0.651.
$$

The complement, $0.9^{10}$, is the probability of missing risky in all ten independent first choices. A positive chance to try an action creates an opportunity, but a particular finite dataset can still miss it. If the policy changes or choices are dependent, this simple calculation needs adjustment.

## What the original Grid World adds

The book compares a 5-by-5 Grid World with $\gamma=0.9$, boundary reward $-1$, forbidden reward $-10$ and target reward $1$. Figure 5.6 holds the highest-probability actions fixed and varies $\epsilon$; in this environment, the values shown fall as $\epsilon$ increases. Figure 5.7 instead chooses the best policy **within each fixed-$\epsilon$ class**. Its preferred action can change as $\epsilon$ changes: for instance, the book explains that random movement near the target can lead into surrounding forbidden cells, making escape preferable at larger $\epsilon$.

Figure 5.8 also shows that visits can be very uneven even in a long trajectory. The distribution depends on the policy, transitions and starting state, so positive action probabilities do not alone ensure that every state is reached or every pair is well sampled. A fixed $\epsilon>0$ constrains the final policy. A decreasing schedule may favor exploitation later, but needs appropriate reachability, continued sampling and further algorithm-specific conditions for convergence.
