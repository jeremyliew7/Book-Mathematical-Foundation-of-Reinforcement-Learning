# 6.2 Robbins–Monro algorithm

Suppose you must tune a machine until its average error is zero, but each reading is affected by unpredictable measurement noise. A single reading cannot reveal the exact error. Can a sequence of small corrections still locate the setting you want?

Robbins–Monro (RM) addresses this kind of root finding. The unknown target $w^*$ satisfies $g(w^*)=0$, where the scalar function $g$ is not directly available. At input $w_k$ you observe $\widetilde g(w_k,\eta_k)=g(w_k)+\eta_k$. Here $\eta_k$ is the observation error; $k$ counts successive observations. RM updates the estimate with a positive step size $a_k$:

$$
w_{k+1}=w_k-a_k\widetilde g(w_k,\eta_k)
=w_k-a_k[g(w_k)+\eta_k].
$$

If $g$ increases through its root, a positive observation suggests moving left and a negative observation suggests moving right. Noise can reverse the suggested direction for an individual update, so the method relies on the cumulative behavior of the observations.

## Follow several corrections

Take $g(w)=w-2$, so the root is $2$. Start at $w_1=0$ and choose the illustrative step sizes $a_1=1/2$, $a_2=1/3$ and $a_3=1/4$. Suppose the successive noise realizations are $0.2$, $-0.3$, and $1.4$.

| $k$ | Current $w_k$ | Noiseless $g(w_k)$ | Observed $\widetilde g$ | Next estimate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0$ | $-2$ | $-1.8$ | $0-\tfrac12(-1.8)=0.9$ |
| 2 | $0.9$ | $-1.1$ | $-1.4$ | $0.9-\tfrac13(-1.4)=1.3667$ |
| 3 | $1.3667$ | $-0.6333$ | $0.7667$ | $1.3667-\tfrac14(0.7667)=1.175$ |

The first two readings move the estimate toward the root. The third reading has the wrong sign because its noise exceeds the true signal, so it moves away. A particular noisy step can be misleading even when the observation errors are centered in the long run. The table rounds the intermediate display; the third calculation uses the unrounded value.

## What makes convergence plausible?

The book's scalar Theorem 6.1 uses a globally increasing function with bounded slope, $0<c_1\leq g'(w)\leq c_2$, and positive step sizes satisfying

$$
\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k=\infty,\qquad \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k^2<\infty.
$$

The lower slope bound supplies a restoring direction and gives a unique root; the upper bound limits how sharply the function changes. The sum conditions balance two needs. An infinite sum leaves an unlimited cumulative capacity to correct persistent error; a finite squared sum limits the cumulative contribution of suitable centered noise. For $a_k=1/k$, the harmonic series diverges and the series of squares converges. By contrast, $a_k=1/k^2$ has too little total step size for this theorem, and a fixed positive $a_k$ fails the squared-sum condition.

The relevant noise is defined relative to the history $\mathcal H_k$ available **before** the next observation. A useful sufficient condition is $\mathbb E[\eta_k\mid\mathcal H_k]=0$ and $\mathbb E[\eta_k^2\mid\mathcal H_k]\leq C$ for one finite constant $C$, alongside the slope and step-size assumptions. Under this strengthened setting, $w_k\to w^*$ almost surely: the limiting statement holds with probability one, although a realized path may move away on some steps.

### Source note: two conditions worth checking

**Noise.** On printed page 106 the book states only $\mathbb E[\eta_k^2\mid\mathcal H_k]<\infty$ for each $k$. This permits the variance to grow without bound. To see the consequence, take $g(w)=w$, $a_k=1/k$, $w_1=0$, and $\eta_k=kZ_k$, where $Z_k$ are independent standard normal variables. Every conditional variance $k^2$ is finite, yet

$$
w_{k+1}=-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^k jZ_j,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(w_{k+1})=\frac{1}{k^2}\sum_{j=1}^k j^2\sim\frac{k}{3}.
$$

The iterates are centered Gaussian with growing variance, so they do not converge to zero even in probability. The uniform second-moment bound in Section 6.3 prevents this example. This is a mathematical annotation to the source statement.

**Step sizes.** Printed page 108 says $a_k=c_k/k$ preserves both sums when $c_k$ is bounded. Boundedness alone is insufficient: $c_k=1/k$ gives $a_k=1/k^2$, whose sum is finite. A positive uniform lower bound $c_k\geq c>0$ and a finite upper bound $c_k\leq C$ are sufficient by comparison with $1/k$ and $1/k^2$.

## Mean estimation is root finding

Let $X$ be a random observation with unknown mean $\mu=\mathbb E[X]$. We want $w^*=\mu$, equivalently the root of $g(w)=w-\mu$. An observation $X_k$ supplies

$$
\widetilde g(w_k)=w_k-X_k
=\underbrace{(w_k-\mu)}_{g(w_k)}+\underbrace{(\mu-X_k)}_{\eta_k}.
$$

Substituting this observation into RM yields $w_{k+1}=w_k+a_k(X_k-w_k)$: take a fraction of the gap between the new sample and the current estimate. With $a_k=1/k$, the first step replaces any initial guess with $X_1$, and induction gives $w_{k+1}=k^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^k X_i$. If the samples are i.i.d. with finite variance and independent of the preceding history, the noise has conditional mean zero and uniformly bounded conditional second moment. This puts the update into the strengthened convergence setting above.

## Try it yourself

1. **Calculate.** If $w_k=1$, $a_k=0.25$, and the noisy observation is $2$, find $w_{k+1}$ and indicate its direction.

   *Hint:* subtract the product of the step size and the observation.
2. **Check a schedule.** Determine which of the two sum conditions holds for $a_k=k^{-3/4}$.

   *Hint:* compare the exponents of $a_k$ and $a_k^2$ with the $p$-series threshold.
3. **Explain an assumption.** If $\eta_k$ has unconditional mean zero but is correlated with the previous estimate, why should you inspect $\mathbb E[\eta_k\mid\mathcal H_k]$?

   *Hint:* conditioning reveals the average error available at the actual update point.
4. **Derive.** For mean estimation, subtract $\mu$ from both sides of the update and separate the old estimation error from the centered sample.

   *Hint:* add and subtract $\mu$ inside $X_k-w_k$.
