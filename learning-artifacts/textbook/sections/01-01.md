# 1.1 A grid world example

Imagine a robot in a nine-cell room. It can move up, down, left, right, or stay in place. Its task is to reach the goal, while avoiding forbidden cells and unnecessary detours. That sounds straightforward—but what exactly must the robot learn?

In this section, you will distinguish a **route** from a **policy**, identify the agent and environment, and see why knowing the rules changes how we solve the problem. No reinforcement-learning background is assumed.

## The room and its decision maker

The book's Figure 1.2 presents a $3\times3$ grid. The following labels make the later state notation visible; the two forbidden cells and target follow the book's Figure 1.3:

| Left | Middle | Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $s_1$ | $s_2$ | $s_3$ |
| $s_4$ | $s_5$ | $s_6$ (forbidden) |
| $s_7$ (forbidden) | $s_8$ | $s_9$ (goal) |

The robot is the **agent**: it chooses an action. The grid, movement rules and feedback are part of the **environment** with which it interacts. Here a state is the robot's current cell; Chapter 1 will define the terms more carefully.

The word “forbidden” identifies an undesirable cell in the example. It does not by itself tell you whether entry is physically impossible. In the book's selected transition rules the robot can enter a forbidden cell, and an associated penalty discourages it. Those rules are introduced in Sections 1.3 and 1.5.

## One route is not yet a policy

Suppose the robot starts at $s_1$. Consider the route

$$
s_1\rightarrow s_2\rightarrow s_5\rightarrow s_8\rightarrow s_9.
$$

Read this as a sequence of visited cells, not as an equation to solve. Each arrow is one move: right, down, down, and right. The route reaches $s_9$ in four moves without entering a forbidden cell. It demonstrates one possible way to act **from this starting position**; it does not by itself tell the robot what to do if it starts at $s_3$ or arrives at a cell not listed in the route.

A **policy** supplies a decision rule for the situations the agent can encounter. For example, a partial policy might say “at $s_1$ move right; at $s_2$ move down; at $s_5$ move down; at $s_8$ move right.” To make this a policy for the whole grid, we must also specify choices in the other states. The exact representation of a policy, including random choices, is developed in Section 1.4.

> **Pause and predict:** If the robot starts at $s_2$ and follows the partial policy above, which cell does it visit next? Can the same partial policy tell you what to do from $s_4$?

The point is not that every policy has to be written as a sentence for each cell. It is that a policy answers a different question from a single observed trajectory: **what choice should be made in each situation?**

## What information is available?

There are two different situations to keep apart:

1. **Known rules.** If the map and movement rules are known, a planner can use them to compare routes or policies. This does not mean the calculation is always easy.
2. **Unknown rules.** If the robot does not know how its choices affect the environment, it must obtain information through interaction. It takes actions, observes what happens, and can revise its decisions. A single interaction does not establish an optimal policy.

Later sections introduce feedback called **reward** and a longer-term measure called **return**. These give a mathematical way to express preferences, but do not assume a particular reward table or claim that a four-step route is globally optimal before those rules and the objective are specified.

## Work through it

1. **Trace:** Start at $s_1$ and follow right, down, down, right. List the visited cells and count the moves. Use the table rather than memorizing the route above.
2. **Compare:** Starting at $s_1$, trace right, right, down, down. Does this route enter a forbidden cell? What extra information would you need before calling one route better than the other?
3. **Transfer:** A robot starts at $s_4$ instead. Why does the route written for $s_1$ not completely specify its next choice? Write one possible action for $s_4$ and explain what additional rules are needed to predict the outcome.

**Hint:** For the second question, keep “can enter,” “is penalized,” and “is impossible to select” as separate claims. The diagram alone does not settle all three.

## Take forward

The agent makes decisions in an environment. A route records one sequence; a policy specifies how to choose actions across situations. To make the problem precise, the next section introduces **state** and **action**; the sections after that define transitions, policy probabilities, and rewards.
