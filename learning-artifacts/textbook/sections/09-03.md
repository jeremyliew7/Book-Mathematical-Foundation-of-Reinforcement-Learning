# 9.3 Derive policy gradients and their state weights

**Source:** [Chapter 9 PDF, pages 8–20](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%209%20Policy%20Gradient%20Methods.pdf#page=8), printed pages 198–210. The numerical example continues [Sections 9.1–9.2](09-01.md).

A policy parameter influences both the current action and future state visits. The policy-gradient theorem organizes both effects into action-value terms weighted by how states are visited. We first derive the discounted fixed-start form, then contrast it with the average-reward form.

Throughout the derivation, consider a finite MDP with bounded rewards, transition and reward laws independent of $\theta$, differentiable policy probabilities, and $0<\gamma<1$. Assume the sums and derivatives may be interchanged. For the log form take $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)>0$ for the actions considered.

## The score trick: from a sum to an expectation

For any positive differentiable action probability, the chain rule gives

$$
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)
=\frac{\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)},
\qquad
\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s)
=\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s).
$$

The vector $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ is called the **score**. Substituting the second identity into a sum gives

$$
\sum_a\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q_\pi(s,a)
=\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q_\pi(s,a)
=\mathbb E_{A\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}
[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A\mid s)q_\pi(s,A)].
$$

The last line uses the definition of expectation over actions. For the shared two-action sigmoid, $p=\pi_\theta(\text{switch}\mid s)$ and $\partial_\theta p=p(1-p)$, so

$$
\partial_\theta\log\pi_\theta(\text{switch}\mid s)=1-p,\qquad
\partial_\theta\log\pi_\theta(\text{stay}\mid s)=-p.
$$

At $p=1/2$ these scores are $+1/2$ and $-1/2$. The score tells how a small parameter change affects the log probability of the sampled action; multiplying by its action value produces a contribution to the expected gradient.

## Why future visits appear in the discounted gradient

Write $v(s)=\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q(s,a)$. Differentiating gives two terms:

$$
\nabla v(s)=\underbrace{\sum_a\nabla\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q(s,a)}_{u(s)}
+\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\nabla q(s,a).
$$

The second term matters because $q$ depends on the future policy. Using $q(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)v(s')$ and the parameter-independent environment,

$$
\nabla v(s)=u(s)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P_\pi(s'\mid s)\nabla v(s').
$$

Each state has a direct effect $u(s)$ and a discounted future effect. Substitute this equation repeatedly. Since $\gamma<1$ and the finite-chain conditions above hold,

$$
\nabla v(s)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t
\sum_{s'}[P_\pi^t]_{ss'}\,u(s').
$$

The $t=0$ term is $u(s)$; the $t=1$ term averages $u$ over next states and discounts once; later terms do the same for later visits. This unrolling accounts for the dependence of $q$ on $\theta$—the final appearance of $q$ as a multiplier does not make it parameter-independent.

## Fixed initial distribution and normalization

Let $S_0\sim d_0$, with $d_0$ independent of $\theta$, and define the **unnormalized discounted occupancy**

$$
\rho_\pi(s)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t
\Pr_\pi(S_t=s\mid S_0\sim d_0).
$$

Weighting the preceding expansion by $d_0$ yields

$$
\nabla J_{d_0}
=\sum_s\rho_\pi(s)\sum_a\nabla\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q_\pi(s,a).
$$

If the continuing chain includes a state at every time step, $\sum_s\rho_\pi(s)=\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t=1/(1-\gamma)$. A terminal episodic model can be represented with an absorbing terminal state so this accounting remains valid. Thus $\rho_\pi$ is a measure, not a normalized probability distribution. Define $d_\gamma=(1-\gamma)\rho_\pi$; then the score form is

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
\nabla J_{d_0}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
\mathbb E_{\substack{S\sim d_\gamma\\A\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid S)}}
[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(A\mid S)q_\pi(S,A)]}.
$$

The book's Theorem 9.2 uses $\rho_\pi$ in an expectation notation. Its displayed $\rho_\pi$ sums to $1/(1-\gamma)$, so when sampling from the normalized distribution $d_\gamma$, the factor above is required.

## Check the formula by hand

Use the two-room task with $\theta=0$, hence $p=1/2$, $\gamma=1/2$, and always start at $A$. Section 9.2 found $v(A)=5/4$, $v(B)=7/4$. At time zero the agent is in $A$; at each later time it is equally likely to be in either room. Therefore,

$$
\rho(A)=1+\sum_{t=1}^\infty(1/2)^t(1/2)=3/2,\quad
\rho(B)=\sum_{t=1}^\infty(1/2)^t(1/2)=1/2.
$$

Their sum is $2$; the normalized weights are $d_\gamma(A)=3/4$, $d_\gamma(B)=1/4$.

| State | $q(\text{stay})$ | $q(\text{switch})$ | $u(s)=\frac14[q(\text{switch})-q(\text{stay})]$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $A$ | $1+\frac12\frac54=\frac{13}{8}$ | $\frac12\frac74=\frac78$ | $-\frac{3}{16}$ |
| $B$ | $\frac12\frac74=\frac78$ | $2+\frac12\frac54=\frac{21}{8}$ | $\frac7{16}$ |

Here $\partial_\theta p=p(1-p)=1/4$, while $\partial_\theta(1-p)=-1/4$. Hence

$$
\partial_\theta J_{d_0}
=\tfrac32(-\tfrac3{16})+\tfrac12(\tfrac7{16})
=-\tfrac1{16}.
$$

Using normalized weights gives the same answer: multiply $(3/4)(-3/16)+(1/4)(7/16)$ by $1/(1-\gamma)=2$. The negative sign means a small increase in $\theta$ locally reduces the discounted value from $A$.

## Average reward uses differential values

For a continuing finite irreducible chain, let $\bar r_\pi=d_\pi^\top r_\pi$ and define a differential state value $h$ through the Poisson equation

$$
h=r_\pi-\bar r_\pi\mathbf1+P_\pi h,\qquad
q^{\mathrm{diff}}(s,a)=r(s,a)-\bar r_\pi+\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)h(s').
$$

Subtracting the average reward centers rewards around the long-run rate. Under the usual differentiability and ergodicity conditions, the average-reward gradient is

$$
\nabla\bar r_\pi
=\sum_s d_\pi(s)\sum_a\nabla\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q^{\mathrm{diff}}(s,a)
=\mathbb E_{S\sim d_\pi,A\sim\pi_\theta}
[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(A\mid S)q^{\mathrm{diff}}(S,A)].
$$

The stationary weights here are normalized; there is no discounted-occupancy factor. To see the key cancellation, differentiating the Poisson relation gives $\nabla h(s)=u_{\rm diff}(s)-\nabla\bar r_\pi+\sum_{s'}P_\pi(s'\mid s)\nabla h(s')$. Multiply by $d_\pi(s)$ and sum: $d_\pi^\top P_\pi=d_\pi^\top$ cancels the two $\nabla h$ terms, leaving $\nabla\bar r_\pi=\sum_s d_\pi(s)u_{\rm diff}(s)$. Establishing existence and differentiability of $h$ and $d_\pi$ is a separate Markov-chain result; the source's Theorem 9.4 and Boxes 9.5–9.6 supply the matrix argument under its assumptions.

In the room at $p=1/2$, $\bar r_\pi=3/4$. One normalized solution is $h(A)=-1/4$, $h(B)=1/4$. The differential action values are $(0,-1/2)$ for (stay, switch) in $A$ and $(-1/2,1)$ in $B$. The two $u_{\rm diff}$ values are $-1/8$ and $3/8$; averaging them with $d_\pi=(1/2,1/2)$ gives $\partial_\theta\bar r_\pi=1/8$. Here average reward increases with $\theta$ even though discounted value from $A$ decreases: the objectives ask different questions.

Adding the same constant to both components of $h$ adds it to each differential action value. Its gradient contribution cancels because $\sum_a\nabla\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=\nabla1=0$.

For the stationary-weighted discounted objective, the book's Theorem 9.3 gives an approximation involving discounted $q$ when $\gamma$ approaches one; it retains conditions on the term arising from the policy-dependent stationary distribution. It should be kept distinct from both exact formulas above.
