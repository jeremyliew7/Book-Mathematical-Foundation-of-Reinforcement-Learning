# 10.2 Advantage actor-critic (A2C)

[Chapter 10 PDF, page 3](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%2010%20Actor-Critic%20Methods.pdf#page=3). Prerequisites: QAC, conditional expectation and TD learning.

## Compare actions with a reference

Suppose a policy tends to find high-return states, so every action value at a state is positive. A sampled QAC step uses a positive multiplier even for a relatively poor action. Can we judge an action relative to what the policy usually achieves at that state while preserving the expected policy-gradient signal?

For a fixed $s$, subtract any action-independent baseline $b(s)$. When the discrete policy has suitable differentiable support,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{A\sim\pi_\theta}[b(s)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A\mid s)]
&=b(s)\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\frac{\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}\\
&=b(s)\nabla_\theta\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the expected score times $q_\pi-b$ equals the expected score times $q_\pi$. The cancellation concerns the score term with the baseline held as a numerical multiplier. It does not generally hold for a baseline that depends on the sampled action.

## Advantage and variance

The natural reference $v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)q_\pi(s,a)$ is the policy-weighted mean action value. The **advantage** is

$$A_\pi(s,a)=q_\pi(s,a)-v_\pi(s).$$

A positive advantage means the action is better than the policy's average at this state. Its immediate reward may still be negative: the comparison concerns expected return.

A baseline preserves the mean of the score estimate, but its choice changes its variance. For the trace of the covariance, Box 10.1 derives the following baseline when the denominator is nonzero:

$$b^*(s)=\frac{\mathbb E_\pi[\|\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A\mid s)\|^2q_\pi(s,A)]}{\mathbb E_\pi[\|\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A\mid s)\|^2]}.$$

The squared score weights matter: the state value baseline is convenient, but it need not be the variance-minimizing choice.

### A complete numerical comparison

At one state let $\pi(L)=0.8$, $\pi(R)=0.2$, $q(L)=2$, and $q(R)=0$. Use a binary logistic policy, so the scalar scores are $0.2$ for $L$ and $-0.8$ for $R$. The state value is $v=0.8(2)+0.2(0)=1.6$.

| Baseline | Estimate if $L$ | Estimate if $R$ | Mean | Variance |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $b=0$ | $0.4$ | $0$ | $0.32$ | $0.0256$ |
| $b=v=1.6$ | $0.08$ | $1.28$ | $0.32$ | $0.2304$ |
| $b=0.4$ | $0.32$ | $0.32$ | $0.32$ | $0$ |

For example, with $b=1.6$ the mean is $0.8(0.08)+0.2(1.28)=0.32$. The same baseline makes this particular estimate more variable: mean preservation alone does not guarantee variance reduction. The score-weighted formula gives $b^*=(0.8\cdot0.2^2\cdot2)/(0.8\cdot0.2^2+0.2\cdot0.8^2)=0.4$.

## Why a TD error can stand in for advantage

Given the true $v_\pi$, the action-value definition gives

$$q_\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E[R_{t+1}+\gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1})\mid S_t=s,A_t=a].$$

Subtracting $v_\pi(s)$ inside this conditional expectation shows that its mean TD error equals $A_\pi(s,a)$. A single observed TD error is a sample of this quantity; replacing $v_\pi$ with $v_w$ introduces approximation error.

The book's A2C uses $\delta_t=r_{t+1}+\gamma v_w(s_{t+1})-v_w(s_t)$, followed by

$$\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t+\alpha_\theta\delta_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t),\qquad w_{t+1}=w_t+\alpha_w\delta_t\nabla_wv_w(s_t).$$

For a concrete transition with $r=1$, $\gamma=0.9$, $v_w(s)=2$, $v_w(s')=2$, the signal is $\delta=0.8$. With left-action score $0.5$ and actor step size $0.1$, the scalar actor parameter rises by $0.04$.
