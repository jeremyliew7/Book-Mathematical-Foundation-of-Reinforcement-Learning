# 4.2 Policy iteration

A robot has a rule for every location, but the rule keeps sending it into a wall. How can we use a known model to replace that rule with a better one? Policy iteration separates the question into two tasks: first evaluate what happens if the robot follows its current rule; then use that evaluation to choose a better action at every state. Repeating these tasks builds a sequence of policies.

We work with a finite discounted MDP, known transition and reward models, bounded rewards, and $0\leq\gamma<1$. You will need the fixed-policy Bellman equation from Chapter 2 and the greedy one-step lookahead from Section 4.1.

## Two levels of iteration

A policy $\pi_k$ assigns action probabilities at each state; $k$ counts the **outer** policy updates. Its value $v_{\pi_k}(s)$ is the expected discounted return when starting at $s$ and following that policy thereafter. For $n$ states, put those values into an $n\times1$ vector $v_{\pi_k}$. The $n\times1$ vector $r_{\pi_k}$ contains expected immediate rewards and the $n\times n$ matrix $P_{\pi_k}$ contains transition probabilities under this policy. Each row of $P_{\pi_k}$ sums to 1.

Define the fixed-policy backup $T_\pi v=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi v$. A backup adds the immediate reward to the discounted expected successor value. Evaluating $\pi_k$ means solving the fixed-point equation

$$
v_{\pi_k}=T_{\pi_k}v_{\pi_k}=r_{\pi_k}+\gamma P_{\pi_k}v_{\pi_k}.
$$

One solution method is $(I-\gamma P_{\pi_k})v_{\pi_k}=r_{\pi_k}$; the inverse exists under the discounted assumptions. Alternatively start with an arbitrary estimate $v_{\pi_k}^{(0)}$ and repeatedly apply

$$
v_{\pi_k}^{(j+1)}=T_{\pi_k}v_{\pi_k}^{(j)},\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots
$$

Here $j$ counts **inner** evaluation steps while $\pi_k$ stays fixed. These estimates converge to $v_{\pi_k}$, but a finite iterate need not be exact. For stochastic policies the state-level backup first averages over actions and then over successor states; evaluation changes the estimate, not the policy.

After evaluation, compare actions using the current policy value. For action $a$ at state $s$,

$$
q_{\pi_k}(s,a)=\sum_r p(r\mid s,a)r+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v_{\pi_k}(s').
$$

The first term is expected immediate reward; the second assumes action $a$ is taken once and $\pi_k$ is followed afterward. Improvement chooses a maximizing action at each state, creating $\pi_{k+1}$. The new policy is then evaluated afresh: the comparison values $q_{\pi_k}$ are not generally the eventual values of following $\pi_{k+1}$ forever.

## Work through the author's two-state model

Figure 4.3 has states $s_1,s_2$ and actions left $a_\ell$, stay $a_0$, right $a_r$. The discount is $\gamma=0.9$. A boundary hit gives $-1$; entering or staying in the target $s_2$ gives $1$; the remaining valid transitions below give $0$. The complete deterministic model, transcribed from the author's Table 4.4, is:

| State | Left: reward, next | Stay: reward, next | Right: reward, next |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $s_1$ | $-1,s_1$ | $0,s_1$ | $1,s_2$ |
| $s_2$ | $0,s_1$ | $1,s_2$ | $-1,s_2$ |

The initial policy $\pi_0$ chooses left in both states. Thus $r_{\pi_0}=(-1,0)^\mathsf{T}$ and $P_{\pi_0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Solving its two equations gives

$$
v_{\pi_0}(s_1)=-1+0.9v_{\pi_0}(s_1)=-10,\qquad
v_{\pi_0}(s_2)=0+0.9v_{\pi_0}(s_1)=-9.
$$

The $-10$ is not a single wall penalty: it is the discounted sum of repeated penalties. Starting evaluation from $(0,0)^\mathsf{T}$ instead, the first three simultaneous updates are $(-1,0)$, $(-1.9,-0.9)$ and $(-2.71,-1.71)$; both entries use the **previous** vector. These are estimates of $(-10,-9)$, not three different policies.

Now use the exact old policy value for a one-step action comparison. For example, right at $s_1$ gives $1+0.9(-9)=-7.1$. All entries are:

| State | $q_{\pi_0}(s,a_\ell)$ | $q_{\pi_0}(s,a_0)$ | $q_{\pi_0}(s,a_r)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $s_1$ | $-10$ | $-9$ | $-7.1$ |
| $s_2$ | $-9$ | $-7.1$ | $-9.1$ |

The largest number in each row selects $\pi_1$: right at $s_1$ and stay at $s_2$. To see the effect of **continuing** with the new policy, evaluate it, rather than stopping at $-7.1$:

$$
v_{\pi_1}(s_2)=1+0.9v_{\pi_1}(s_2)=10,\qquad
v_{\pi_1}(s_1)=1+0.9v_{\pi_1}(s_2)=10.
$$

A further greedy check using $(10,10)$ gives action values $(8,9,10)$ at $s_1$ and $(9,10,8)$ at $s_2$. The same policy is greedy with respect to its own value, so its value satisfies the optimality equation; under the stated discounted assumptions it is optimal. This last evaluation and greedy check extend the author's example. The improvement occurred in one outer update **for this model**, not necessarily for another MDP. The author's larger Figure 4.4 illustrates multiple policy updates; proximity to the target there reflects its particular rewards and transitions, not a general distance rule.

## Why an exact greedy improvement cannot hurt

Let $v=v_{\pi_k}$ and let $\pi'=\pi_{k+1}$ be greedy with respect to this **exact** value. Since the old action is among the choices, the greedy backup obeys

$$
T_{\pi'}v\geq T_{\pi_k}v=v
$$

componentwise. Let $d=v-v_{\pi'}$. The Bellman equation for $v_{\pi'}$ and the inequality above give

$$
d\leq T_{\pi'}v-T_{\pi'}v_{\pi'}=\gamma P_{\pi'}d.
$$

A transition matrix has nonnegative entries, so multiplying an inequality by it preserves componentwise order. Repeat to get $d\leq\gamma^nP_{\pi'}^n d$. Every row of $P_{\pi'}^n$ is a probability distribution, so each component on the right has absolute value at most $\gamma^n\lVert d\rVert_\infty$, which goes to zero. Hence $d\leq0$: the new policy value is at least as large at every state. The argument starts with **exact** $v_{\pi_k}$; replacing it by an arbitrary inaccurate estimate does not automatically preserve that guarantee.

The book establishes convergence of policy values to $v^*$. Its statement about convergence of the policy sequence needs a tie rule: if several actions are equally good, arbitrarily alternating between them can change the displayed policy forever while its value remains unchanged. Retaining the current action whenever it is a maximizer is one standard way to avoid needless switching.

   *Hint:* compare the transition rules used in each backup.
