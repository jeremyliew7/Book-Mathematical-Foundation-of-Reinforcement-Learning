# 1.7 Markov decision processes

[Chapter 1 PDF, pages 11–12](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%201%20Basic%20Concepts.pdf#page=11), book pages 11–12; only text before Section 1.8 on page 12 belongs to this section. Prerequisites: Sections 1.2–1.6. The corridor and probability tables below are original examples.

## What must we specify before predicting a step?

Suppose a robot is in corridor state $A$. If we know only that its action is **go**, we still cannot say with certainty whether it reaches $B$: the environment may be stochastic. To calculate the probability of the next state or reward, we need a model; to calculate which action is taken, we also need a policy.

An MDP specifies the decision setting and its dynamics. A policy can then be used within that setting. The source lists these pieces in a discrete setting:

| Symbol | Meaning | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathcal S$ | Possible states | What can be represented |
| $\mathcal A(s)$ | Legal actions at state $s$ | What the agent may choose |
| $\mathcal R(s,a)$ | Possible rewards after $(s,a)$ | What feedback can occur |
| $p(s'\mid s,a)$ | Probability of next state $s'$ given $s,a$ | Environment model |
| $p(r\mid s,a)$ | Probability of reward $r$ given $s,a$ | Environment model |
| $\pi(a\mid s)$ | Probability the policy selects $a$ at $s$ | Agent's decision rule |

For each legal $(s,a)$, the transition probabilities sum to one over $s'$ and the reward probabilities sum to one over $r$. For each $s$, the policy probabilities sum to one over legal $a$. These are three different normalizations; a policy probability is not an environment transition probability.

## One fixed policy, one calculated transition probability

Continue the corridor example from [Section 1.6](01-06.md). For the original model below, once the terminal state $T$ is reached, stop the episode; the $T\to T$ line is its optional zero-reward absorbing extension.

| Current state and action | Next state and reward | Probability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $(A,\mathrm{go})$ | $(B,0)$ | $0.8$ |
| $(A,\mathrm{go})$ | $(A,-1)$ | $0.2$ |
| $(A,\mathrm{wait})$ | $(A,0)$ | $1$ |
| $(B,\mathrm{go})$ | $(T,2)$ | $1$ |
| $(T,\mathrm{wait})$ | $(T,0)$ | $1$ |

Suppose a stationary policy at $A$ chooses go with probability $0.75$ and wait with probability $0.25$, and at $B$ chooses go with probability $1$. What is the probability of reaching $B$ on the next step, *before* seeing which action is chosen?

1. The probability of choosing go and then reaching $B$ is $0.75\times0.8=0.6$.
2. Choosing wait has probability $0.25$, but reaches $B$ with probability $0$.
3. The two action cases are disjoint, so their probabilities add: $0.6+0.25(0)=0.6$.

In general, for a fixed state-only policy,

$$P_\pi(s'\mid s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}\pi(a\mid s)p(s'\mid s,a).$$

Here $P_\pi(B\mid A)=0.6$ and $P_\pi(A\mid A)=0.4$, which sum to one. This is the transition rule of the induced state process. Averaging does not make an individual transition deterministic: in one run, the next state is still one outcome.

## What does Markov actually mean?

The source writes, for the next-state distribution,

$$p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t,s_{t-1},a_{t-1},\ldots)=p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t),$$

and gives a separate analogous condition for the next reward distribution. These equations say that, **once current state and action are given**, the listed earlier history adds no predictive information for those respective distributions. History may have shaped the current state; Markov does not mean the past never mattered.

Consider a robot whose collision probability depends on both position and velocity. If two histories produce the same recorded position but different unrecorded velocities, position alone may give different next-step probabilities. The state representation may need velocity as well. Whether adding it is sufficient depends on the actual dynamics; it is not guaranteed merely because we added one variable.

## Marginals do not determine the joint distribution

The transition and reward rules listed above are **marginals**. A fuller model can specify $p(s',r\mid s,a)$: the probability of a particular next-state and reward pair. For $(A,\mathrm{go})$ in our corridor, the table says

$$p(B,0\mid A,\mathrm{go})=0.8.$$

Its marginals are $p(B\mid A,\mathrm{go})=0.8$ and $p(0\mid A,\mathrm{go})=0.8$. Multiplying them gives $0.64$, not $0.8$: reaching $B$ and receiving reward $0$ are correlated in this constructed model. Conditional independence is an extra assumption, not a consequence of knowing the marginals.

To see why the distinction matters, the following **different** joint distribution has the same marginals:

| Outcome after $(A,\mathrm{go})$ | Alternative joint probability |
| --- | --- |
| $(B,0)$ | $0.6$ |
| $(B,-1)$ | $0.2$ |
| $(A,0)$ | $0.2$ |
| $(A,-1)$ | $0$ |

Both models have $p(B\mid A,\mathrm{go})=0.8$ and $p(0\mid A,\mathrm{go})=0.8$, yet assign different probability to $(B,0)$. Consequently, separate Markov conditions for the two marginals do not by themselves prove that their **joint** distribution is independent of earlier history: its coupling could change with history while each marginal stays unchanged. When a result needs the joint kernel, state and check that stronger assumption rather than quietly treating the source's two marginal equations as proof of it.

## Stationary dynamics are not a fixed policy

A stationary environment model uses the same transition and reward mechanisms at different times. The agent could still change its policy from one round to another. Conversely, fixing a state-only policy produces the displayed $P_\pi$ when the transition kernel is stationary. The book mainly considers finite stationary MDPs; a time-varying model needs an explicitly time-dependent rule or another appropriate representation.

## Try it yourself

1. **Recognize.** Which corridor probability belongs to the policy: $0.75$ for choosing go, or $0.8$ for reaching $B$ after go? *Hint:* ask what is conditioned on in each statement.
2. **Apply.** Change the probability of choosing go at $A$ to $0.5$, keeping the same dynamics. Find $P_\pi(B\mid A)$. *Hint:* add the go and wait cases separately.
3. **Reason.** In the first joint table, compare $p(B,0\mid A,\mathrm{go})$ with $p(B\mid A,\mathrm{go})p(0\mid A,\mathrm{go})$. What would equality require? *Hint:* conditional independence is a statement about the joint, not just its two marginals.
4. **Extend.** If the wind changes transition probabilities by time of day while the policy remains fixed, which part of the model is nonstationary? *Hint:* inspect the conditional transition rule, not the policy table.
