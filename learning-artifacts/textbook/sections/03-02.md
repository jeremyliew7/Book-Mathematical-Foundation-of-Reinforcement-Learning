# 3.2 What does “optimal” mean at every state?

[Chapter 3 PDF, pages 3–4](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%203%20Optimal%20State%20Values%20and%20Bellman%20Optimality%20Equation.pdf#page=3), printed pages 37–38. Section 3.2 starts partway through page 3 and ends before Section 3.3. Prerequisites: $v_\pi(s)$ and the policy comparison in [Section 3.1](03-01.md). The example below is original; no algorithm was executed.

## Why a single initial state is not the whole question

Imagine two routes, and suppose one is better if the robot starts on the left while the other is better if it starts on the right. The answer to “which route is better?” depends on the initial state. The book therefore compares the values **state by state** under the same MDP, reward definition, and discount factor.

For policies $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$, the source's no-worse relation is

$$v_{\pi_1}(s)\ge v_{\pi_2}(s)\quad\text{for every }s\in\mathcal S.$$

This condition permits equality. One higher component is not enough if another component is lower; the policies may be incomparable under this relation. Nor can values be compared meaningfully if one policy was evaluated with a different reward function or discount.

## A worked comparison in an explicit MDP

Consider two states $x,y$ with discount $\gamma=0.5$. Every action returns to the same state; the difference is its immediate reward:

| Current state | Red action reward | Blue action reward | Next state for either action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x$ | $1.5$ | $1$ | $x$ |
| $y$ | $0.5$ | $1$ | $y$ |

Let $\pi_R$ choose Red at both states, and let $\pi_B$ choose Blue at both. For a self-loop with reward $r$, the fixed-policy equation is $v=r+0.5v$, so $v=2r$. Therefore

$$v_{\pi_R}=(3,1),\qquad v_{\pi_B}=(2,2),$$

in state order $(x,y)$. Red wins at $x$ because $3>2$; Blue wins at $y$ because $2>1$. Neither vector is componentwise at least the other. This does **not** mean a policy that is optimal at both states cannot exist.

Indeed, in this particular MDP let $\pi^*$ choose Red at $x$ and Blue at $y$. Its values are $(3,2)$. Because actions leave their current state unchanged, choosing the largest immediate reward at each state also maximizes every future term at that state. Thus this constructed policy is optimal here. The existence of an optimal policy for the broader finite discounted class requires the argument in Section 3.4; it does not follow merely from finding one in this toy example.

## Definition first, existence later

The book defines $\pi^*$ to be optimal if, for **every** state $s$ and every allowed comparison policy $\pi$,

$$v_{\pi^*}(s)\ge v_\pi(s).$$

Its associated state values are written $v^*(s)$. Read the two “for every” statements carefully: optimal from one selected start state is weaker than the book's all-state criterion. Also, a definition says *what would count* as an optimal policy; it does not itself prove that one exists or tell us how to find it.

For finite states and actions, a stationary MDP, bounded rewards, and $0<\gamma<1$, the later Bellman argument establishes an optimal deterministic stationary policy. Do not extend that existence statement automatically to arbitrary infinite spaces or undiscounted objectives.

## Unique value does not mean unique behavior

In the example, imagine adding a Green action at $y$ with the same reward $1$ and self-transition as Blue. A policy choosing Red at $x$ can choose either Blue or Green at $y$ and still have values $(3,2)$. Randomizing between these two tied actions at $y$ also preserves that value. Thus different policies can share one optimal value vector. A tie alone only supports this conclusion when the actions are optimal under the relevant model and remaining choices are also optimal.

## Try it yourself

1. **Recognize.** In the table, which policy is better from $x$, and which is better from $y$? Does either dominate the other? *Hint:* compare the first components and then the second components.
2. **Apply.** With $\gamma=0.5$, a self-loop gives reward $0.3$ every step. Write and solve its fixed-policy value equation. *Hint:* start from $v=0.3+0.5v$.
3. **Reason.** A policy is best from a given initial state. What extra comparison would be required to establish the source's all-state definition? *Hint:* inspect both quantifiers in the definition.
4. **Extend.** If Blue and Green tie at one state but a policy is suboptimal at another, is that policy necessarily optimal overall? *Hint:* a local tie does not remove the all-state requirement.
