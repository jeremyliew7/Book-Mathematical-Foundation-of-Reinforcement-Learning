# 9.2 Choose the policy objective

**Source:** [Chapter 9 PDF, pages 3–8](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%209%20Policy%20Gradient%20Methods.pdf#page=3), printed pages 193–198. The example continues the two-room task in [Section 9.1](09-01.md).

A gradient optimizes a number. A policy can be judged by the discounted return from its starting state, by a weighted average of values, or by long-run reward per step. Specify this objective before choosing its gradient.

## Fix the policy and compute its values

The rooms $A,B$ have stay/switch rewards $1,0$ at $A$ and $0,2$ at $B$; staying keeps the room and switching changes it. Let the shared switch probability be $p=1/2$ and $\gamma=1/2$. The transition matrix and expected immediate reward vector are

$$
P_\pi=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&1/2\\1/2&1/2\end{bmatrix},\qquad
r_\pi=\begin{bmatrix}1/2\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Rows of $P_\pi$ describe the next-room probabilities; the first immediate reward is $(1/2)1+(1/2)0$, and the second is $(1/2)0+(1/2)2$. Bellman's equations give

$$
v(A)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\bigl[\tfrac12v(A)+\tfrac12v(B)\bigr],\quad
v(B)=1+\tfrac12\bigl[\tfrac12v(A)+\tfrac12v(B)\bigr].
$$

Subtracting gives $v(B)-v(A)=1/2$; substitution yields $v(A)=5/4$ and $v(B)=7/4$.

## Objective 1: discounted value from specified starts

For a fixed initial distribution $d_0$,

$$
J_{d_0}(\theta)=\sum_s d_0(s)v_{\pi_\theta}(s)
=\mathbb E_{\substack{S_0\sim d_0\\\pi_\theta}}
\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^tR_{t+1}\right].
$$

The first equality weights each state's value by the chance of starting there; the second is the law of total expectation. If the task always begins in $A$, set $d_0(A)=1$ and $d_0(B)=0$, giving $J_{d_0}=5/4$. For an equal initial mixture the same policy instead has $J_{d_0}=3/2$. The weights express which starting situations matter.

## Objective 2: stationary-weighted value and average reward

A stationary distribution satisfies $d_\pi^\top P_\pi=d_\pi^\top$ and sums to one. For this example $d_\pi(A)=d_\pi(B)=1/2$. Its stationary-weighted discounted value and average reward per step are

$$
\bar v_\pi=\tfrac12v(A)+\tfrac12v(B)=\tfrac32,\qquad
\bar r_\pi=\tfrac12r_\pi(A)+\tfrac12r_\pi(B)=\tfrac34.
$$

For a finite chain with the required long-run conditions, $\bar r_\pi$ also equals the limit of the expected time-average reward. In this example each action has positive probability and the induced two-state chain is irreducible and aperiodic. More generally, a stationary distribution's existence alone does not guarantee that the state distribution converges to it from every start.

The Bellman equation $v_\pi=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi v_\pi$, multiplied by $d_\pi^\top$, gives

$$
\bar v_\pi=\bar r_\pi+\gamma\bar v_\pi,
\quad\text{so}\quad
\bar r_\pi=(1-\gamma)\bar v_\pi.
$$

The middle step uses $d_\pi^\top P_\pi=d_\pi^\top$. In the room, $(1-1/2)(3/2)=3/4$. The equality uses **stationary** weighting of discounted values, not an arbitrary fixed start: $(1-1/2)J_{d_0}$ from start $A$ is $5/8$, not $3/4$.

## Why policy dependence matters

For general $\theta$, the stationary distribution can itself change when the policy changes. Differentiating $\sum_s d_{\pi_\theta}(s)v_{\pi_\theta}(s)$ then involves changes in both factors. In our symmetric two-room parameterization the stationary distribution happens to remain $(1/2,1/2)$ for every finite $\theta$; this is a special property of this example.

**Checkpoint:** Which objective would you choose to optimize performance when every trial begins at $A$? Which would describe continuing reward per step?
