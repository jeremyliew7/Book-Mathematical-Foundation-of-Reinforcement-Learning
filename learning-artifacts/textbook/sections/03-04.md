# 3.4 From the Bellman fixed point to an optimal policy

[Chapter 3 PDF, pages 12–14](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%203%20Optimal%20State%20Values%20and%20Bellman%20Optimality%20Equation.pdf#page=12), printed pages 46–48. We assume finite state and action sets, stationary dynamics, bounded rewards, and $0<\gamma<1$. Prerequisites: the operator, maximum, and contraction argument in [Section 3.3](03-03.md).

## Three questions, not one

Suppose we write the Bellman optimality equation $v=Tv$. Three claims must be distinguished:

1. Does the equation have a solution, and is that solution unique?
2. Can a policy be built whose actual value equals that solution?
3. Is that policy at least as good as every competitor from every state?

Calling a vector a *fixed point* answers only the equation. Calling a policy *greedy* describes a choice rule for a supplied value. The proof must still connect those objects to the all-state optimality definition in [Section 3.2](03-02.md).

## First: obtain the unique fixed point

Section 3.3 shows that $T$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in the infinity norm. Its fixed point $v^*$ therefore exists and is unique, and the iteration

$$v_{k+1}=Tv_k$$

converges to it from any initial vector. The guarantee concerns the limit: a finite number of updates gives an approximation unless exact equality is separately established. This result alone says the fixed point is unique; the policy link comes next.

## Then: make a greedy decision using the fixed point

For each state and legal action, use the true fixed point to calculate

$$q^*(s,a)=\sum_r p(r\mid s,a)r+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v^*(s').$$

Pick any maximizing action $a^*(s)\in\arg\max_a q^*(s,a)$ at each state. A deterministic policy $\pi_g$ selects that action with probability one. Because the chosen action attains the maximum in every state,

$$T_{\pi_g}v^*=Tv^*=v^*,$$

where $T_\pi v=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi v$ is the **fixed-policy** Bellman operator. Its unique fixed point is the actual fixed-policy value $v_{\pi_g}$. Since $v^*$ is also a fixed point of $T_{\pi_g}$, uniqueness gives $v_{\pi_g}=v^*$.

This step answers question 2: the greedy policy achieves the candidate fixed-point vector. It uses the true $v^*$, not merely an arbitrary estimate $v_k$.

## Why is that value at least as good as another policy?

First compare with any fixed stationary policy $\pi$. The maximum defining $T$ cannot be smaller than that policy's weighted action average, so

$$v^*=Tv^*\ge T_\pi v^*=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi v^*.$$

Policy evaluation also gives $v_\pi=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi v_\pi$. Subtract this equality from the inequality and write $d=v^*-v_\pi$:

$$d\ge\gamma P_\pi d.$$

The comparison is componentwise. Since $P_\pi$ has nonnegative entries, multiplying an existing componentwise inequality by $P_\pi$ preserves its direction. Repeating gives

$$d\ge\gamma P_\pi d\ge\gamma^2P_\pi^2d\ge\cdots\ge\gamma^nP_\pi^nd.$$

A row-stochastic matrix cannot increase the infinity norm, hence $\|\gamma^nP_\pi^nd\|_\infty\le\gamma^n\|d\|_\infty\to0$. This limit argument works even before we know the signs of the components of $d$; it yields $d\ge0$, or $v^*\ge v_\pi$ for every state.

The book states optimality against all policies. For a possibly time-varying or history-dependent competitor, the same one-step maximum bounds its conditional expected reward plus $\gamma v^*$ at each step. Iterating for $n$ steps bounds the competitor's expected discounted $n$-step rewards plus $\gamma^n v^*(S_n)$ by $v^*(s)$. Bounded rewards and $v^*$ make the discounted tail vanish as $n\to\infty$, yielding the same comparison. This supplies the bridge from the displayed stationary-policy calculation to the broader all-policy statement in the finite discounted setting.

## Check the two-state model by hand

Return to the original teaching model from [Section 3.1](03-01.md): at $A$, Left gives reward $1$ and reaches $B$, while Right gives $0.5$ and returns to $A$; at $B$, Wait gives $0.2$ and stays at $B$. Let $\gamma=0.5$.

The $B$ fixed-point equation is $v^*(B)=0.2+0.5v^*(B)$, so $v^*(B)=0.4$. For the candidate $v^*(A)=1.2$, the action scores are

$$q^*(A,\mathrm{Left})=1+0.5(0.4)=1.2,\qquad q^*(A,\mathrm{Right})=0.5+0.5(1.2)=1.1.$$

Left is maximizing, and Wait is the only action at $B$. Thus the candidate $(1.2,0.4)$ satisfies both state equations, and the corresponding deterministic greedy policy attains it. Its optimality in this model follows from the proven finite discounted result.

## Unique value, multiple policies

The source's Figure 3.3 illustrates that $v^*$ can be unique even when several actions tie at a state. Different deterministic policies can choose different tied maximizing actions; randomization supported only on maximizing actions also satisfies the greedy equation. A policy placing probability on a strictly inferior action at some state is not made optimal merely by a tie elsewhere.

A common temptation is to take a finite-step vector $v_k$, choose an action greedy for $v_k$, and call that policy exactly optimal. The contraction bound guarantees that the estimate approaches $v^*$, but an approximation does not by itself prove the action choice is correct: action-score gaps and the estimate error matter.

## Try it yourself

1. **Recognize.** Which uniqueness result lets us conclude $v_{\pi_g}=v^*$ after finding $T_{\pi_g}v^*=v^*$? *Hint:* it is a fixed-policy result, not merely the uniqueness of $T$'s fixed point.
2. **Apply.** In the two-state model, if $v^*(B)=0.4$, calculate Left's score at $A$. *Hint:* use its immediate reward and next state.
3. **Reason.** Why can $d\ge\gamma P_\pi d$ imply $d\ge0$ without assuming $d\ge0$ first? *Hint:* bound the norm of the right-hand side after $n$ repetitions.
4. **Extend.** Suppose two actions at one state tie for the optimal score. What policies might still be optimal, and why would a strictly worse action not become optimal because of that tie? *Hint:* examine the weighted average at each state.
