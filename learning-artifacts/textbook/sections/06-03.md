# 6.3 Dvoretzky's convergence theorem

In the mean-estimation update, each observation can push the estimate away from the mean. Why can the accumulated errors nevertheless disappear? This optional mathematical section studies that question through an error recursion. It also gives a way to recognize the same structure in Robbins–Monro and, later, TD learning.

Let $\Delta_k$ be the current estimate minus its target. The book studies

$$
\Delta_{k+1}=(1-\alpha_k)\Delta_k+\beta_k\eta_k.
$$

The coefficient $\alpha_k\geq0$ controls correction of the existing error; $\beta_k\geq0$ scales new noise $\eta_k$. If $0<\alpha_k<1$ and there is no noise, the magnitude contracts on that step. A single noise term may undo the gain, so we need assumptions over the whole sequence.

For example, when $\Delta_k=4$, $\alpha_k=\beta_k=0.25$, and $\eta_k=-2$, the next error is $(0.75)(4)+(0.25)(-2)=2.5$. Had the noise been $+8$, the next error would instead be $5$: a particular observation can make the estimate worse.

## The assumptions and their roles

Let $\mathcal H_k$ denote information available before $\eta_k$ is observed, including the current error and past noise. In the predictable-coefficient setting used below, $\alpha_k$ and $\beta_k$ are determined by this history. The book's theorem uses the following conditions, interpreted almost surely for random sequences:

$$
\sum_k\alpha_k=\infty,\quad \sum_k\alpha_k^2<\infty,\quad
\sum_k\beta_k^2<\infty,\quad
\mathbb E[\eta_k\mid\mathcal H_k]=0,\quad
\mathbb E[\eta_k^2\mid\mathcal H_k]\leq C.
$$

The first sum provides sustained correction. Square summability and the uniform noise-moment bound limit cumulative noise. Conditional mean zero says that, given what is already known, the next noise does not systematically push in one direction. These conditions lead to $\Delta_k\to0$ almost surely under the theorem's remaining technical requirements.

History matters: an unconditional mean of zero can conceal a bias known from the past. Also, if $\beta_k$ depends on the *current* noise, $\mathbb E[\beta_k\eta_k\mid\mathcal H_k]$ need not be zero. For a concrete example, let $\eta_k$ be equally likely to be $+1$ or $-1$ independently of history and choose $\beta_k=1$ when $\eta_k=1$ and $0$ otherwise. Then $\mathbb E[\eta_k\mid\mathcal H_k]=0$ but $\mathbb E[\beta_k\eta_k\mid\mathcal H_k]=1/2$. The coefficient has selected the favorable sign.

## Why squared error reveals the mechanism

Set $h_k=\Delta_k^2$. Expand the recursion before taking expectations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
h_{k+1}-h_k
&=[(1-\alpha_k)\Delta_k+\beta_k\eta_k]^2-\Delta_k^2\\
&=-\alpha_k(2-\alpha_k)\Delta_k^2
 +2(1-\alpha_k)\beta_k\Delta_k\eta_k+\beta_k^2\eta_k^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Condition on $\mathcal H_k$. Since the coefficients and $\Delta_k$ are known at this point, they can be taken outside the conditional expectation. The middle term disappears because the conditional mean of $\eta_k$ is zero:

$$
\mathbb E[h_{k+1}-h_k\mid\mathcal H_k]
=-\alpha_k(2-\alpha_k)\Delta_k^2
+\beta_k^2\mathbb E[\eta_k^2\mid\mathcal H_k].
$$

Once $\alpha_k$ is small enough, the first term pulls expected squared error down, while the second is the variance cost of noise. The book uses a quasimartingale result to complete the argument: under the needed hypotheses, squared error has a limit and the accumulated correction is finite. If that limiting squared error were positive, the divergent $\sum\alpha_k$ would make the accumulated correction infinite, a contradiction. This is the role of the convergence theorem beyond the displayed algebra.

## Connect two familiar algorithms

For mean estimation let $\mu=\mathbb E[X]$, $\Delta_k=w_k-\mu$, and $w_{k+1}=w_k+\alpha_k(X_k-w_k)$. Subtracting $\mu$ gives

$$
\Delta_{k+1}=(1-\alpha_k)\Delta_k+\alpha_k(X_k-\mu).
$$

Thus $\beta_k=\alpha_k$ and $\eta_k=X_k-\mu$. With i.i.d. finite-variance observations independent of the previous history, the conditional noise mean is zero and its second moment is the fixed variance. The step-size conditions then supply the other ingredients.

For Robbins–Monro, set $\Delta_k=w_k-w^*$ and use $g(w^*)=0$. The mean-value theorem gives $g(w_k)-g(w^*)=g'(\xi_k)\Delta_k$ for some $\xi_k$ between $w_k$ and $w^*$. Therefore

$$
\Delta_{k+1}=[1-a_kg'(\xi_k)]\Delta_k+a_k(-\eta_k).
$$

This is the same recursion with $\alpha_k=a_kg'(\xi_k)$, $\beta_k=a_k$, and noise $-\eta_k$. If $0<c_1\leq g'\leq c_2$, the step-size sums transfer by comparison. The conditional noise and predictability requirements must also be checked, including the strengthened moment condition discussed in [Section 6.2](06-02.md).

The book then gives a finite-component extension in Theorem 6.3. Instead of one scalar error, it controls a vector of errors using a maximum norm and permits a conditional noise mean bounded in terms of the current error. This is the bridge used in Chapter 7, where each state has its own interacting value estimate.

## Try it yourself

1. **Compute.** Calculate the next error for $\Delta_k=4$, $\alpha_k=0.25$, $\beta_k=0.25$, and $\eta_k=2$.

   *Hint:* calculate the retained old error and the new noise contribution separately.
2. **Derive.** Starting from the mean update, identify $\Delta_k$, $\alpha_k$, $\beta_k$, and $\eta_k$ in the general recursion.

   *Hint:* subtract $\mu$ and insert $\mu-\mu$ in the sample term.
3. **Challenge predictability.** For the sign-dependent coefficient above, calculate $\mathbb E[\beta_k\eta_k]$ and explain why knowing only $\mathbb E[\eta_k]=0$ does not make the cross term vanish.

   *Hint:* list the two equally likely outcomes.
4. **Connect conditions.** Why does a finite sum $\sum_k\alpha_k\Delta_k^2$ imply a zero limiting squared error only when a limit exists and $\sum_k\alpha_k$ diverges?

   *Hint:* suppose the limit were a positive number and bound the tail from below.
