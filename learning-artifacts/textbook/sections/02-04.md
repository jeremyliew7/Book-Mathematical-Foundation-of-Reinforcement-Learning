# 2.4 Bellman equation

## Source and scope

[Chapter 2 PDF, physical pages 6–8](../../../3%20-%20Chapter%202%20State%20Values%20and%20Bellman%20Equation.pdf#page=6), corresponding to printed pages 20–22. The derivation follows the source's fixed-policy Bellman equation. The worked example is constructed for this companion; it is not the author's Grid World example or a program run.

## Learning objectives and prerequisites

After this section, you should be able to explain why a value can depend on another value, derive the expectation equation in stages, read each sum, and solve a small pair of value equations. You need the definition of discounted return and state value from [Section 2.3](02-03.md), conditional expectation, and the distinction between policy and environment transition.

For the derivation below, take a finite state and action space, a stationary environment, a fixed stationary policy $\pi$, bounded rewards, and $0\leq\gamma<1$. These assumptions make the discounted return well-defined and let a state's value be independent of the time index. Other settings exist, but require their own conditions.

## The problem

Suppose you want to evaluate a robot's **current policy**, not improve it yet. From state $s$, the robot first chooses an action, receives a reward, and lands in a next state. Then the same policy continues. You could average the discounted return of many complete trajectories. But if you already know the policy and environment model, can the value of $s$ be expressed using the values of possible next states?

That is the Bellman equation's question. It turns one long-term quantity into a relationship between a one-step reward and another long-term quantity. It does not say which action is best.

## The concept

Imagine pausing a trajectory immediately after its first transition. Everything already received is the immediate reward; everything after the pause is the return from the next time step. The discount factor $\gamma$ controls the contribution of that future part.

| Symbol | Meaning in this section |
| --- | --- |
| $S_t=s$ | The current random state has the particular value $s$. |
| $A_t=a$ | The policy selects action $a$ at time $t$. |
| $R_{t+1}=r$ | The immediate reward produced by the transition. |
| $S_{t+1}=s'$ | A possible next state, not necessarily a different state. |
| $\pi(a\mid s)$ | Probability of selecting $a$ in $s$ under the fixed policy. |
| $p(s',r\mid s,a)$ | Joint probability of next state $s'$ and reward $r$, given $s,a$. |
| $G_t$ | The discounted sum of rewards from time $t+1$ onward. |
| $v_\pi(s)$ | The expected $G_t$ conditional on starting at $s$ and subsequently following $\pi$. |

The policy supplies action probabilities; the environment supplies reward and transition probabilities. A single trajectory has one realized return, while $v_\pi(s)$ averages possible returns. Keep those two levels separate throughout the derivation.

**Reading map:** split one return → take its conditional expectation → average over actions and outcomes → solve the resulting equations together. As you read, ask which step changes a realized quantity into an average. If that is unclear, return to Step 2 before expanding any sums.

## Derivation and examples

### Step 1: Split one trajectory's return

Start from the definition. The first line expands the return; the second factors $\gamma$ out of everything after the first reward:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G_t&=R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\gamma^2R_{t+3}+\cdots\\
&=R_{t+1}+\gamma\left(R_{t+2}+\gamma R_{t+3}+\cdots\right)\\
&=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is an identity for each trajectory for which the return is defined. It is not yet an expectation or an equation for a state value.

### Step 2: Average over trajectories starting at $s$

By definition, $v_\pi(s)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s]$. Substitute the identity above and use linearity of conditional expectation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_\pi(s)
&=\mathbb E_\pi[R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}\mid S_t=s]\\
&=\mathbb E_\pi[R_{t+1}\mid S_t=s]
+\gamma\,\mathbb E_\pi[G_{t+1}\mid S_t=s].
\end{aligned}
$$

The first expectation is the mean immediate reward. The second is the mean future return, viewed from before we know the next state. They can be added whether or not the two quantities are independent: linearity does not require independence.

### Step 3: Condition on the action and next state

First, the law of total expectation separates the possible actions. For each chosen action, average the possible rewards:

$$
\mathbb E_\pi[R_{t+1}\mid S_t=s]
=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}\pi(a\mid s)
\sum_r p(r\mid s,a)\,r.
$$

The policy weight is outside the reward sum because it chooses the action before the environment produces a reward.

**Pause and predict:** if the policy assigns zero probability to one available action, what does that action contribute to this particular policy's value? Keep your answer separate from the question of whether that action would have a value if chosen. For the future term, condition on the next state. Given $S_{t+1}=s'$, the Markov property and the same fixed subsequent policy let us replace the expected future return by $v_\pi(s')$:

$$
\mathbb E_\pi[G_{t+1}\mid S_t=s]
=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}\pi(a\mid s)
\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\,v_\pi(s').
$$

Here $p(r\mid s,a)$ and $p(s'\mid s,a)$ are **marginals** of the joint environment model. Substitute both expressions into Step 2:

$$
\boxed{v_\pi(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}\pi(a\mid s)
\left[\sum_r p(r\mid s,a)r
+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v_\pi(s')\right].}
$$

Read it from inside out: for one action, average its reward and possible next-state values; then average those action results under $\pi$. There is one such equation for every state. The next-state values on the right are not known just because they are written there; together the equations form a system.

### Step 4: See why reward and next state can be dependent

The source also gives a joint-distribution version:

$$
v_\pi(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}\pi(a\mid s)
\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\left[r+\gamma v_\pi(s')\right].
$$

The joint probability keeps each reward paired with its next state. Expanding the bracket and summing over the other variable produces the two marginal expectations in Step 3. We did **not** replace the joint probability by $p(s'\mid s,a)p(r\mid s,a)$; that product would impose conditional independence, which is not needed here.

### Step 5: Work through a two-state example

The following is an **original hand example**. Both $A$ and $B$ are continuing, nonterminal states, $\gamma=0.5$, and the rewards are deterministic conditional on each listed outcome:

| Current state | Action | Policy probability | Environment outcome: next state, reward, probability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $A$ | `rest` | $0.5$ | $(A,0)$ with probability $1$ |
| $A$ | `try` | $0.5$ | $(A,0)$ with probability $0.5$; $(B,2)$ with probability $0.5$ |
| $B$ | `wait` | $1$ | $(B,1)$ with probability $1$ |

**First solve $B$.** It always receives $1$ and returns to $B$, so

$$v_\pi(B)=1+0.5v_\pi(B),\qquad v_\pi(B)=2.$$

**Now set up $A$.** If the policy chooses `rest`, the one-step quantity inside the brackets is $0+0.5v_\pi(A)$. For `try`, the mean immediate reward is $0.5(0)+0.5(2)=1$, and the mean next-state value is $0.5v_\pi(A)+0.5v_\pi(B)$. Weight the two actions by their policy probabilities:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_\pi(A)
&=0.5\,[0+0.5v_\pi(A)]\\
&\quad+0.5\,[1+0.5(0.5v_\pi(A)+0.5v_\pi(B))]\\
&=0.375v_\pi(A)+0.75.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $0.625v_\pi(A)=0.75$ and $v_\pi(A)=1.2$. As a check, at these values `rest` has one-step quantity $0.6$ and `try` has $1.8$; their policy-weighted mean is $0.5(0.6)+0.5(1.8)=1.2$.

The `try` outcomes also show dependence: given $(A,\texttt{try})$, $P(S'=B,R=2)=0.5$, while $P(S'=B)P(R=2)=0.5\cdot0.5=0.25$. The marginal-sum form still works because it adds expected reward and expected future value; it never assumes those outcomes are independent.

## Applications and limitations

**Check your understanding before moving on:** cover the boxed equation. Can you reconstruct its three ingredients—policy choice, immediate reward, and next-state value—without recalling their exact order from memory? Reopen the equation and check which probability weights each ingredient.

This equation performs **policy evaluation**: $\pi$ stays fixed while we solve for its values. Replacing the policy-weighted action average by a maximization changes the problem to optimality and needs the Bellman optimality equation discussed later.

For the finite discounted case, the equations can be written as $(I-\gamma P_\pi)v_\pi=r_\pi$ and solved together. The example assumes its transition and reward probabilities are known. Model-free methods later estimate values from samples instead. If action sets vary by state, use $\mathcal A(s)$; continuous spaces require integrals and the corresponding existence conditions. In a finite-horizon or nonstationary setting, values generally need a time index.

## Exercises

1. **Identify:** In the boxed equation, which probability is controlled by the policy, and which two describe the environment?
2. **Calculate:** In the worked example, keep everything else fixed but let $\gamma=0$. Write the resulting equations for $v_\pi(A)$ and $v_\pi(B)$ and solve them.
3. **Explain:** In the `try` action, reward and next state are dependent. Why is it still legitimate to add their separate expectations?
4. **Extend:** Suppose the policy at $A$ chooses `try` with probability $0.8$ and `rest` with probability $0.2$. Write the two Bellman equations before solving them. Identify which quantities change and which environment probabilities do not.

Do not look for an answer key here before attempting the questions. To check your reasoning, show the equation you set up and explain each weight.

## Key terms and misconceptions

| Term | Meaning | Common misconception |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Return $G_t$ | Discounted future rewards for one trajectory | It is automatically the expected value. |
| State value $v_\pi(s)$ | Conditional expectation of return under a fixed policy | It gives the reward of the very next step only. |
| Policy evaluation | Finding values for the given policy | It necessarily selects the maximizing action. |
| Conditional expectation | Average over outcomes given stated information | It can be replaced by an arbitrary observed outcome. |
| Marginal expectation | Average of one component of a joint outcome | Adding marginal expectations requires reward and next state to be independent. |
