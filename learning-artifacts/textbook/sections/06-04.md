# 6.4 Stochastic gradient descent

Imagine estimating the average reward of an unfamiliar process. You can receive one observation at a time, but cannot calculate the population average in advance. Rather than wait for every possible observation, you can revise your estimate after each one. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) expresses this idea as an optimization method.

Let $w$ be a parameter, $X$ a random observation and $f(w,X)$ the loss on one observation. The expected objective is $J(w)=\mathbb E[f(w,X)]$. When differentiation and expectation can be interchanged, $\nabla J(w)=\mathbb E[\nabla_w f(w,X)]$. Exact gradient descent subtracts this expected gradient; SGD uses a sample $X_k$ instead:

$$
w_{k+1}=w_k-\alpha_k\nabla_w f(w_k,X_k).
$$

Here $\alpha_k>0$ is the learning rate. The sample gradient is a random direction; its conditional mean equals the true gradient when $X_k$ has the required distribution independently of the current history. A sample can point uphill, but the average direction can still point downhill.

## Derive mean estimation as SGD

Choose the scalar squared loss $f(w,X)=\tfrac12(w-X)^2$. If $\mu=\mathbb E[X]$ and $\mathbb E[X^2]<\infty$, then

$$
J(w)=\tfrac12\mathbb E[(w-X)^2],\qquad
J'(w)=\mathbb E[w-X]=w-\mu.
$$

The derivative vanishes at $w=\mu$, and $J''(w)=1>0$, so the mean is the unique minimizer. We do not know $\mu$, but a sample supplies $f'(w_k,X_k)=w_k-X_k$. Substitution gives

$$
w_{k+1}=w_k-\alpha_k(w_k-X_k)
=(1-\alpha_k)w_k+\alpha_k X_k.
$$

For $w_k=4$, $X_k=2$ and $\alpha_k=0.25$, the sample gradient is $2$ and $w_{k+1}=4-0.25(2)=3.5$. With the next sample $X_{k+1}=6$ and the same illustrative rate, the gradient is $3.5-6=-2.5$, giving $w_{k+2}=4.125$. The estimate moves toward each sample, and different samples can pull in opposite directions.

To connect this directly to Robbins–Monro, write

$$
\nabla_w f(w_k,X_k)=\underbrace{\nabla J(w_k)}_{g(w_k)}+
\underbrace{[\nabla_w f(w_k,X_k)-\nabla J(w_k)]}_{\eta_k}.
$$

SGD is RM root finding for $g(w)=\nabla J(w)$ when the sample gradient is conditionally unbiased. A zero gradient is a stationary point; whether it is a minimum depends on the objective. For the strongly convex quadratic above, the stationary point is the unique minimum.

## One sample, a minibatch, or the whole dataset?

For data $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, define the empirical objective $J_n(w)=n^{-1}\sum_i f(w,x_i)$. At the same current $w$, full-batch gradient descent averages all $n$ gradients; SGD uses one uniformly sampled index; a minibatch averages $m$ sampled gradients. Uniform independent sampling makes the sample average conditionally unbiased for the empirical gradient.

Take data $(0,2,4,6)$ and $w=4$. For the squared loss the four gradients are $(4,2,0,-2)$, so the full-batch gradient is $1$. A minibatch drawing indices for values $(0,4)$ gives $(4+0)/2=2$, whereas two draws of the same value $0$ give $4$. Both are possible when sampling with replacement; even a batch of four draws can repeat an index and omit another. A batch containing each item exactly once reproduces the full-batch gradient.

For independent, identically distributed gradient samples with finite variance $\sigma^2$, averaging $m$ samples gives variance $\sigma^2/m$ at a fixed $w$. More samples also cost computation, and correlations or sampling without replacement change the variance calculation. The book's Figure 6.5 illustrates one trajectory comparison; actual wall-clock and accuracy trade-offs depend on the setting.

## What is required for convergence?

The book's scalar Theorem 6.4 uses positive bounded curvature, $0<c_1\leq f''(w,X)\leq c_2$, i.i.d. samples and learning rates with $\sum_k\alpha_k=\infty$ and $\sum_k\alpha_k^2<\infty$. The reduction to RM also needs the interchange of derivative and expectation and appropriate conditional noise-moment control; i.i.d. sampling alone does not supply every moment assumption. See the source annotation in [Section 6.2](06-02.md).

A constant rate is useful for tracking a changing target, but it does not satisfy the square-summability condition. For a fixed noisy target, the estimate can continue to fluctuate. For example, at the exact mean of a nondegenerate data distribution, the expected gradient is zero while an individual sample gradient can still be nonzero; the next SGD step can move away.

## A bridge to TD

In mean estimation, the sample $X_k$ is the target and the correction is $\alpha_k(X_k-w_k)$. In one-step TD learning, the target for the visited state is $R_{t+1}+\gamma v_t(S_{t+1})$, so the correction has the same target-minus-current form. The important new feature is that the target itself includes the current estimate of a successor state: it changes as the value table changes. [Section 7.1](07-01.md) derives the update from the Bellman equation and explains why this is called bootstrapping.

   *Hint:* differentiate $\tfrac12(w-X_k)^2$ before applying the update.
2. **Compare.** For the four data values in the example, calculate the full-batch gradient at $w=2$ and the minibatch gradient for samples $0$ and $6$.

   *Hint:* at a common $w$, average the individual $w-x_i$ values.
3. **Reason.** At the true mean, can a single sample gradient be nonzero even though the expected gradient is zero? Give a two-outcome example.

   *Hint:* use two equally likely values on opposite sides of their mean.

   *Hint:* consider how the value of the next state enters the Bellman equation.
